## 1. What Are Materials?

Materials are the substances used to make objects. Wood, plastic, metal, glass, rubber, cloth, and paper are common materials. We study materials by observing and testing their properties.

Examples:

- A chair may be made of wood, plastic, or metal.
- A raincoat may be made of plastic or waterproof fabric.

Non-example:

- A chair is an object, not a material.

Common misconception:

- Students sometimes think the object and the material are the same thing.

## 2. Observable Properties of Materials

Scientists describe materials using properties that can be observed or tested. These properties help us compare and classify materials fairly.

### Texture

Texture tells how a material feels.

- Cotton feels soft.
- Sandpaper feels rough.

**Careful:** Color and texture are not the same thing.

### Hardness

Hardness tells if a material is hard or soft.

- Stone is hard.
- Sponge is soft.

**Careful:** Heavy does not always mean hard.

### Flexibility

Flexibility tells how easily a material bends.

- Rubber bends easily.
- A ceramic plate is rigid and not flexible.

**Careful:** Thin does not always mean flexible.

### Absorbency

Absorbency tells whether a material soaks up liquid.

- Tissue and towel absorb water.
- Plastic does not absorb water.

**Careful:** A surface can get wet without soaking up water.

### Magnetism

Magnetism tells whether a material is attracted to a magnet.

- Iron nails and many steel paper clips are magnetic.
- Wood, cloth, and glass are not magnetic.

**Careful:** Not all metals are magnetic.

### Solubility

Solubility tells whether a material dissolves in water.

- Salt and sugar dissolve in water.
- Sand does not dissolve in water.

**Careful:** Dissolving is not the same as disappearing.

### Conductivity

Conductivity tells whether heat or electricity can pass through a material.

- Metals are often used inside electric wires because they conduct electricity.
- Rubber is used on the outside of wires because it is a poor conductor.

**Careful:** Shiny does not always mean conductive.

## 4. Classifying Materials

To classify means to group materials using shared properties. Classification should be based on evidence, not guessing.

You can classify materials in many ways:

- absorbent and non-absorbent
- magnetic and non-magnetic
- soluble and insoluble
- flexible and rigid

Examples:

- Cloth and tissue can be grouped as absorbent.
- Glass and wood can be grouped as non-magnetic.

Non-example:

- Grouping things because they "look nice together."

> [!EXAMPLE] Example Classification
>
> If sponge and paper towel both soak up water, they belong in the absorbent group because the evidence from the water test matches the rule.

## 6. Helpful and Harmful Materials

A material may be useful in one setting and harmful in another. We should think about how and where it is used.

Examples:

- Glass is useful for windows because you can see through it, but broken glass is harmful.
- Bleach helps clean surfaces, but it is harmful if handled carelessly.
- Water is helpful for drinking, but floodwater can be dangerous.
- Plastic containers are useful for storage, but plastic waste can harm the environment.

Important idea:

- The material does not magically change from good to bad. The use, condition, and setting matter.

## 7. Claim and Evidence

In science, a strong answer includes a claim and evidence.

Claim example:

- Paper towel is more absorbent than plastic.

Evidence example:

- In the water test, the paper towel soaked up water while the plastic did not.

## 8. Worked Examples

### Example 1: Sorting by Absorbency

You tested paper towel, plastic, and cloth with a few drops of water. Paper towel and cloth soaked up water. Plastic did not.

**Model answer**

- Absorbent: paper towel, cloth
- Non-absorbent: plastic

**Why it is correct**

- The grouping matches the evidence from the water test.

### Example 2: Reading a Property Table

Material A is smooth, hard, not absorbent, and conductive. Material B is soft, absorbent, and not conductive. Which material is more suitable for a towel?

**Model answer**

- Material B is more suitable for a towel.

**Why it is correct**

- Towels need absorbency, and Material B is absorbent.

### Example 3: One Material, Different Uses

Glass is used for windows and bottles, but broken glass on the floor is dangerous. Is glass beneficial or harmful?

**Model answer**

- Glass can be beneficial or harmful depending on how and where it is used.

## 12. Likely Misconceptions

- Not all metals are magnetic.
- Getting wet is not the same as being absorbent.
- Dissolving is not the same as disappearing.
- A material can be helpful in one setting and harmful in another.
