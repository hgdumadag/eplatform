- household materials can still be unsafe if used carelessly.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Acid | A substance with acidic properties; usually low pH and turns blue litmus red | Vinegar, lemon juice, hydrochloric acid |
| Base | A substance with basic properties; usually high pH and turns red litmus blue | Soap solution, baking soda solution, sodium hydroxide |
| Salt | An ionic compound often formed when an acid reacts with a base | Sodium chloride, saline solution |
| Indicator | A substance that changes color depending on acidity or basicity | Litmus paper, red cabbage juice |
| pH | A scale from 0 to 14 that describes how acidic or basic a solution is | pH 3 is acidic; pH 11 is basic |
| Neutralization | A reaction between an acid and a base that can form water and a salt | Acid + base -> salt + water |

## Main Concept Explanation

### 1. Acids

Acids are substances that show acidic properties in water. Many common food acids taste sour, but **taste is not a safe test** in science class. Instead, use indicators.

Common acid evidence:

- pH less than 7
- turns **blue litmus red**
- red cabbage indicator becomes pink or red
- examples: vinegar, lemon juice, calamansi juice, and hydrochloric acid in a laboratory setting

### 2. Bases

Bases are substances that show basic properties in water. Some bases feel slippery or taste bitter, but those are not safe tests either.

Common base evidence:

- pH greater than 7
- turns **red litmus blue**
- red cabbage indicator becomes green, blue-green, or yellow
- examples: soap solution, baking soda solution, and sodium hydroxide in a laboratory setting

### 3. Salts

In chemistry, a salt is not only table salt. A salt is often an ionic compound formed when an acid reacts with a base.

The general pattern is:

**acid + base -> salt + water**

Example:

**hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide -> sodium chloride + water**

Sodium chloride is table salt. A salt solution can be neutral, acidic, or basic depending on the salt, but Grade 10 work often begins with neutral examples such as sodium chloride solution.

### 4. Indicators

An indicator does not "know" the name of the substance. It responds to the acidity or basicity of the solution by changing color. Your job is to connect the color evidence to the correct classification.

Think of it like this:

| Observation | Strongest simple conclusion |
|---|---|
| Blue litmus turns red | The sample is acidic |
| Red litmus turns blue | The sample is basic |
| Red and blue litmus do not change | The sample may be neutral or too weak for litmus to show clearly |
| Red cabbage turns pink | The sample is acidic |
| Red cabbage turns greenish | The sample is basic |

## Key Idea Box callout

> [!IMPORTANT] Key Idea
>
> Indicators classify substances by **observable color changes**, not by guessing from the substance name. Low pH means acidic, pH 7 is neutral, and high pH means basic. A salt can form when an acid and a base react.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Litmus Test

A student places blue litmus paper into vinegar. The paper turns red.

**Reasoning:**

1. Blue litmus turning red is evidence for an acid.
2. Vinegar is a common household acid.
3. Therefore, the sample is classified as **acidic**.

### Example 2: Red Cabbage Indicator

A safe sample of baking soda dissolved in water is mixed with a few drops of red cabbage indicator. The mixture turns greenish.

**Reasoning:**

1. Red cabbage indicator turns greenish in a base.
2. Baking soda solution is mildly basic.
3. Therefore, the sample is classified as **basic**.

### Example 3: Neutralization Pattern

Hydrochloric acid reacts with sodium hydroxide.

**Word equation:**

**hydrochloric acid + sodium hydroxide -> sodium chloride + water**

**Reasoning:**

- The acid contributes one part.
- The base contributes another part.
- The products include a **salt** and **water**.

## Misconception Alerts

> [!WARNING] Common Traps
>
> **Trap 1:** "All salts are table salt." In chemistry, many ionic compounds are salts.
>
> **Trap 2:** "Neutral means useless." Neutral substances still matter. Pure water and many salt solutions are important in living systems and laboratories.
>
> **Trap 3:** "If it is safe at home, it is safe to mix." Household chemicals can react dangerously. Never mix cleaners or unknown substances.
>
> **Trap 4:** "One indicator gives the whole story." Litmus gives a simple acid/base result, but pH paper or universal indicator gives more detail.

## Error Analysis

Read the student claim, then diagnose the mistake.

**Student claim:** "The liquid must be a base because it did not turn blue litmus red."

**What went wrong?**

Blue litmus staying blue does not prove a base. It could be basic, neutral, or a weak sample that does not show a clear change. A better test is to also use red litmus. If red litmus turns blue, then the sample is basic.

**Corrected reasoning:**

- Blue litmus turns red -> acid.
- Red litmus turns blue -> base.
- No change in either paper -> likely neutral or needs a more sensitive indicator.

## Final Summary

Acids, bases, and salts can be identified using evidence instead of guessing. Acids usually have pH below 7 and turn blue litmus red. Bases usually have pH above 7 and turn red litmus blue. Neutral substances have pH near 7 and may show little or no litmus change. Indicators such as litmus, universal indicator, and red cabbage juice make these differences visible through color changes. When an acid reacts with a base, neutralization can produce water and a salt.
