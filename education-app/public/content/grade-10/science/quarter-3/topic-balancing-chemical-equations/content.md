## What You Should Already Know

Before starting, recall these ideas:

- A **reactant** is a starting substance on the left side of the arrow.
- A **product** is a new substance on the right side of the arrow.
- A chemical formula shows the kinds and numbers of atoms in one particle of a substance.
- The **Law of Conservation of Mass** says atoms are not created or destroyed in a chemical reaction.
- An equation is balanced when each element has the same number of atoms before and after the reaction.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning in this lesson |
|---|---|
| Balanced equation | A chemical equation with equal numbers of each kind of atom on both sides |
| Coefficient | A large number in front of a formula showing how many particles or formula units are present |
| Subscript | A small number in a formula showing how many atoms of an element are in one particle |
| Reactant | A starting substance written on the left side of the arrow |
| Product | A substance formed by the reaction, written on the right side of the arrow |
| Atom count | The total number of atoms of each element on one side of an equation |

## Main Concept Explanation

### 1. Why equations must balance

Chemical reactions rearrange atoms. They do not create new atoms or destroy old ones. A balanced equation is a record that the same atoms are present before and after the reaction.

For every element, ask:

1. How many atoms are on the reactant side?
2. How many atoms are on the product side?
3. Which coefficient can make the smaller side catch up?

### 2. Coefficients change amounts, not identities

You may change a coefficient because it changes how many particles are reacting.

`H2O` means one water molecule.

`2H2O` means two water molecules.

The substance is still water.

You may not change a subscript because it changes the chemical formula.

`H2O` is water.

`H2O2` is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance.

### 3. A reliable balancing strategy

Use this order for many simple equations:

1. Write the correct formulas first.
2. Count atoms of each element on both sides.
3. Balance one element at a time using coefficients.
4. If hydrogen and oxygen appear in several places, save them for last when possible.
5. Recount every atom after each change.
6. Reduce coefficients to the smallest whole-number ratio if needed.

## Key Idea Box

> [!IMPORTANT] Key Idea
>
> Balance equations by changing coefficients only. Never change subscripts. A correct balanced equation has the same atom count for every element on both sides of the arrow.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Balance `H2 + O2 -> H2O`

Start with an atom count.

| Step | Equation | H atoms left | O atoms left | H atoms right | O atoms right | What changed? |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Start | `H2 + O2 -> H2O` | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1 | Oxygen is not balanced |
| Balance O | `H2 + O2 -> 2H2O` | 2 | 2 | 4 | 2 | Put 2 before water |
| Balance H | `2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O` | 4 | 2 | 4 | 2 | Put 2 before hydrogen |

Final answer:

`2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O`

Check: left side has 4 H and 2 O. Right side has 4 H and 2 O.

### Example 2: Balance `Mg + O2 -> MgO`

Oxygen is paired as `O2` on the left but appears one at a time in `MgO` on the right.

| Step | Equation | Mg left | O left | Mg right | O right |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Start | `Mg + O2 -> MgO` | 1 | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| Balance O | `Mg + O2 -> 2MgO` | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Balance Mg | `2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO` | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 |

Final answer:

`2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO`

### Example 3: Balance `N2 + H2 -> NH3`

Balance nitrogen first, then hydrogen.

| Step | Equation | N left | H left | N right | H right |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Start | `N2 + H2 -> NH3` | 2 | 2 | 1 | 3 |
| Balance N | `N2 + H2 -> 2NH3` | 2 | 2 | 2 | 6 |
| Balance H | `N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3` | 2 | 6 | 2 | 6 |

Final answer:

`N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3`

### Example 4: Balance `CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O`

This is methane combustion. Carbon is already balanced, so balance hydrogen next. Save oxygen for last.

| Step | Equation | C left | H left | O left | C right | H right | O right |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Start | `CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O` | 1 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| Balance H | `CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O` | 1 | 4 | 2 | 1 | 4 | 4 |
| Balance O | `CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O` | 1 | 4 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 4 |

Final answer:

`CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O`

## Misconception Alerts

> [!WARNING] Common Traps
>
> **Trap 1:** Changing `H2O` to `H2O2` is not balancing. It changes water into a different substance.
>
> **Trap 2:** Balancing only one element is not enough. Recount every element after each coefficient change.
>
> **Trap 3:** A missing coefficient means `1`, not `0`.
>
> **Trap 4:** Bigger coefficients are not always better. Use the smallest whole-number set that balances the equation.

## Error Analysis

A student tries to balance water formation like this:

`H2 + O2 -> H2O2`

The student says, "Now there are 2 H and 2 O on both sides."

What is wrong?

The atom count matches, but the product formula was changed from `H2O` to `H2O2`. That changes the substance from water to hydrogen peroxide. The correct move is to use coefficients:

`2H2 + O2 -> 2H2O`

## Final Summary

Balancing chemical equations is a careful counting process. The formulas tell you what substances are involved, so their subscripts must stay fixed. Coefficients tell you how many particles take part, so those are the numbers you adjust. When the atom count of every element is equal on both sides, the equation is balanced and follows conservation of mass.
