# Single and Double Replacement Reactions

> [!GOAL] Lesson Goal
>
> By the end of this lesson, you should be able to recognize single and double replacement reactions and explain which element, ion, or group changes partners.

## Estimated Time and Difficulty

| Estimated Time | Difficulty | Main Skill |
|---|---|---|
| 40 minutes | Intermediate | Classifying reactions by tracking partner changes |

## What You Should Already Know

Before starting, check that these ideas feel familiar:

- A **reactant** is a starting substance, written on the left side of the arrow.
- A **product** is a new substance, written on the right side of the arrow.
- A chemical equation shows atoms rearranging, not disappearing.
- An **element** has one kind of atom, such as Zn, Fe, Cu, or Ag.
- A **compound** has two or more elements bonded together, such as HCl, CuSO4, or NaCl.
- In a compound made of ions, a positive part and a negative part stay paired until a reaction changes the pairing.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Memory Cue |
|---|---|---|
| Single replacement | One element replaces another element in a compound. | One outsider swaps into one compound. |
| Double replacement | Two compounds exchange partners. | Two pairs switch partners. |
| Reactivity | How easily a substance takes part in a reaction. | More reactive elements can replace less reactive ones. |
| Precipitate | An insoluble solid that forms from a reaction in solution. | A solid "falls out" of the mixture. |
| Spectator ion | An ion that does not really change partners in the net reaction. | Present, but not the main actor. |
| Ion group | A charged group of atoms that often moves as one unit, such as NO3 or SO4. | Keep the group together. |

## Main Concept Explanation

### Single Replacement: One Outsider Enters

A **single replacement reaction** has this pattern:

`A + BC -> AC + B`

One element, `A`, starts alone. It replaces another element, `B`, inside the compound `BC`. The product is a new compound, `AC`, plus the released element `B`.

Example:

`Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2`

Zinc starts as a free element. It replaces hydrogen in hydrochloric acid. The new compound is zinc chloride, and hydrogen gas is released.

Another example:

`Fe + CuSO4 -> FeSO4 + Cu`

Iron replaces copper in copper(II) sulfate. The sulfate group, `SO4`, stays together and moves with iron.

### Double Replacement: Two Compounds Switch Partners

A **double replacement reaction** has this pattern:

`AB + CD -> AD + CB`

Two compounds react. Their positive and negative parts exchange partners. This often happens in water solution.

Example:

`AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3`

Silver nitrate and sodium chloride exchange partners. Silver pairs with chloride to form silver chloride, `AgCl`, which is a solid precipitate.

Double replacement reactions often have a visible clue:

| Clue | What Forms | Example Product |
|---|---|---|
| Cloudy solid appears | precipitate | AgCl |
| Bubbles appear | gas | CO2 |
| Acid and base react | water and a salt | H2O and NaCl |

### How to Classify Quickly

Use this three-step check:

1. Count the starting substances.
2. Look for a single free element.
3. Track the partner change.

| If you see... | Likely Type | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Element + compound -> compound + element | Single replacement | `A + BC -> AC + B` |
| Compound + compound -> compound + compound | Double replacement | `AB + CD -> AD + CB` |
| One compound breaking apart | Decomposition | `AB -> A + B` |
| Substances combining into one product | Synthesis | `A + B -> AB` |

## Key Idea Box callout

> [!IMPORTANT] Key Idea Box
>
> Single replacement is **one outsider replacing one partner**. Double replacement is **two compounds switching partners**. Do not classify by the number of products alone; classify by how the atoms or ion groups rearrange.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Classify `Fe + CuSO4 -> FeSO4 + Cu`

**Step 1: Identify the reactants.** Fe is a free element. CuSO4 is a compound.

**Step 2: Track the switch.** Fe takes the place of Cu. The sulfate group, SO4, stays together.

**Answer:** This is a **single replacement** reaction.

Pattern match: `A + BC -> AC + B`

### Example 2: Classify `AgNO3 + NaCl -> AgCl + NaNO3`

**Step 1: Identify the reactants.** Both reactants are compounds.

**Step 2: Track the switch.** Ag pairs with Cl, and Na pairs with NO3.

**Answer:** This is a **double replacement** reaction.

Pattern match: `AB + CD -> AD + CB`

### Example 3: Identify the Released Element

Reaction:

`Mg + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2`

Mg replaces H in HCl. Hydrogen is released as `H2`.

**Answer:** The released element is hydrogen.

## Misconception Alerts

> [!WARNING] Misconception Alert 1
>
> "Two products means double replacement" is not reliable. Single replacement also has two products. Check whether the reactants are **element + compound** or **compound + compound**.

> [!WARNING] Misconception Alert 2
>
> Polyatomic ion groups often stay together. In `CuSO4`, the sulfate group `SO4` usually moves as one unit during this kind of classification.

> [!WARNING] Misconception Alert 3
>
> A single replacement reaction usually depends on reactivity. A more reactive element can replace a less reactive one, but a less reactive element may not replace a more reactive one.

## Error Analysis

A student classified this reaction as double replacement:

`Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2`

**Student reason:** "There are two products, so it is double replacement."

**What went wrong?** The student counted products but did not track partners. The reactants are one element plus one compound. Zinc replaces hydrogen, so the reaction follows `A + BC -> AC + B`.

**Correct classification:** Single replacement.

Now analyze this one:

`KCl + AgNO3 -> KNO3 + AgCl`

If someone says this is single replacement because silver moves, what should you correct? Both reactants are compounds, and two partner pairs are rearranged. This is double replacement.

## Final Summary

Single replacement reactions involve one free element replacing another element in a compound. The pattern is `A + BC -> AC + B`.

Double replacement reactions involve two compounds exchanging partners. The pattern is `AB + CD -> AD + CB`.

The best classification strategy is to track relationships: who starts alone, who is paired, and who changes partners after the arrow.
