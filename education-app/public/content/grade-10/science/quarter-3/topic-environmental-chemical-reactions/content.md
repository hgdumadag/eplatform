# Chemical Reactions in the Environment

> [!GOAL] Learning Goal
>
> By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain how combustion, acids reacting with metals, and acids reacting with carbonates affect the natural and built environment. You will use word equations, visible evidence, and local examples such as transport fuels, coastal limestone, concrete structures, galvanized roofs, monuments, and acid rain in polluted air.

## What You Should Already Know

Before this lesson, review these ideas:

- A **chemical reaction** forms one or more new substances.
- Signs of a reaction may include gas bubbles, temperature change, light, color change, odor, or a precipitate.
- **Acids** have low pH and can react with some metals and carbonates.
- **Reactants** are starting substances; **products** are substances formed.
- A **word equation** shows the reaction using names instead of formulas.

Prerequisite link: Lesson 4 introduced single replacement reactions. Acid + metal reactions are a common environmental example of that pattern.

## Main Concept Explanation

### 1. Combustion: fuel reacts with oxygen

Combustion happens when a substance reacts rapidly with oxygen and releases energy as heat and usually light.

**General pattern for complete combustion of a hydrocarbon fuel:**

fuel + oxygen &rarr; carbon dioxide + water + energy

| Everyday example | Reactants | Main products | Helpful use | Environmental concern |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LPG stove | LPG + O<sub>2</sub> | CO<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O | Cooking food | Adds CO<sub>2</sub> to air |
| Jeepney, bus, or motorcycle fuel | Gasoline/diesel + O<sub>2</sub> | CO<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O | Transportation | CO<sub>2</sub>, smoke, NO<sub>x</sub>, particulates |
| Charcoal grilling | Carbon-rich fuel + O<sub>2</sub> | CO<sub>2</sub> + heat | Cooking | Smoke; incomplete combustion can form CO |

In the Philippines, combustion is common in cooking, transport, power generation, and open burning. It is useful because it releases energy. It can be harmful because carbon dioxide contributes to climate change and incomplete combustion can release carbon monoxide, soot, and other pollutants.

> [!WARNING] Safety Note
>
> Carbon monoxide is a poisonous gas that can form when fuel burns with too little oxygen. Never burn charcoal, gasoline, or LPG in a closed room.

### 2. Acids on metals: corrosion and hydrogen gas

Many metals react with acids. The pattern is:

acid + metal &rarr; salt + hydrogen gas

Example:

zinc + hydrochloric acid &rarr; zinc chloride + hydrogen gas

Zn + 2HCl &rarr; ZnCl<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>

### 3. Acids on carbonates: bubbles and surface loss

Carbonates are found in limestone, marble, coral fragments, shells, and eggshells. Acids react with carbonates using this pattern:

acid + carbonate &rarr; salt + water + carbon dioxide

Example:

hydrochloric acid + calcium carbonate &rarr; calcium chloride + water + carbon dioxide

2HCl + CaCO<sub>3</sub> &rarr; CaCl<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O + CO<sub>2</sub>

| Carbonate material | Where you may see it | What acid does |
|---|---|---|
| Limestone | Rock formations, building stone, cement raw material | Slowly dissolves or weakens the surface |
| Marble | Floors, monuments, markers, decorative stone | Loses shine and becomes etched |
| Shells and coral skeletons | Coastal areas and marine environments | Can weaken under acidic conditions |
| Eggshell | Home demonstration material | Fizzes with vinegar because CO<sub>2</sub> forms |

### 4. Acid rain damage: from air pollutants to damaged surfaces

Acid rain forms when pollutants such as sulfur dioxide (SO<sub>2</sub>) and nitrogen oxides (NO<sub>x</sub>) react with oxygen and water in the atmosphere. These pollutants can come from vehicle exhaust, power plants, factories, and burning fuels.

The rain is not strong laboratory acid, but repeated exposure matters. Over months or years, acidic water can corrode metals and react with carbonate-containing stones.

| Step | Process | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Fuel burns in vehicles, factories, or power plants | SO<sub>2</sub> and NO<sub>x</sub> may enter the air |
| 2 | Pollutants react with oxygen and water vapor | Acidic substances form in clouds |
| 3 | Rain, mist, or dry particles reach surfaces | Metals corrode faster; carbonate stone dissolves slowly |
| 4 | Repeated cycles of wetting and drying occur | Buildings, monuments, roofs, and statues become stained, pitted, or weakened |

## Key Idea Box callout

> [!IMPORTANT] Key Idea Box
>
> Environmental chemical reactions rearrange atoms just like laboratory reactions. The important patterns are:
>
> - **Combustion:** fuel + oxygen &rarr; carbon dioxide + water + energy
> - **Acid + metal:** acid + metal &rarr; salt + hydrogen gas
> - **Acid + carbonate:** acid + carbonate &rarr; salt + water + carbon dioxide
>
> The same reaction can be useful, harmful, or both depending on the context.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Complete combustion of methane

Methane is the main component of natural gas.

**Word equation:** methane + oxygen &rarr; carbon dioxide + water + energy

**Formula equation:** CH<sub>4</sub> + 2O<sub>2</sub> &rarr; CO<sub>2</sub> + 2H<sub>2</sub>O

**Meaning:** The fuel reacts with oxygen. Energy is released, so it can cook food or heat water. The products include carbon dioxide, which is linked to climate change when released in large amounts.

### Example 2: Acid on metal

A zinc strip is placed in dilute hydrochloric acid.

**Word equation:** zinc + hydrochloric acid &rarr; zinc chloride + hydrogen gas

**Formula equation:** Zn + 2HCl &rarr; ZnCl<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>

**Evidence:** Bubbles form because hydrogen gas is produced. The metal slowly dissolves because zinc atoms become part of zinc chloride.

### Example 3: Acid on carbonate

Vinegar is placed on eggshell or crushed shell.

**Pattern:** acid + carbonate &rarr; salt + water + carbon dioxide

**Evidence:** Fizzing happens because carbon dioxide gas forms. This is the same pattern that helps explain why acidic rainwater can slowly damage limestone or marble.

## Misconception Alerts

> [!WARNING] Common Traps
>
> - **Trap 1:** "All bubbles mean boiling." Bubbles can also be gases from chemical reactions, such as H<sub>2</sub> or CO<sub>2</sub>.
> - **Trap 2:** "Combustion only means firewood burning." Fuels in engines, stoves, and power plants also undergo combustion.
> - **Trap 3:** "Acid rain melts buildings instantly." Real acid rain is weak, but repeated exposure can slowly damage materials.
> - **Trap 4:** "Rusting and acid + metal are the same reaction." They are related corrosion processes, but rusting specifically involves iron reacting with oxygen and water. Acids can speed corrosion.

## Final Summary

Environmental chemistry is chemistry happening around you. Combustion gives energy for cooking, travel, and electricity, but it also releases carbon dioxide and sometimes harmful pollutants. Acids can react with metals to form salts and hydrogen gas, contributing to corrosion problems. Acids can react with carbonates to form salts, water, and carbon dioxide, explaining fizzing with shells or chalk and long-term damage to limestone or marble. Acid rain connects air pollution to material damage because pollutants can become acidic substances in rainwater.
