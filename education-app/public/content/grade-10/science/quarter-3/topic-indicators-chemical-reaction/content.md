## What You Should Already Know

Before starting, recall these ideas:

- Matter can be described by observable properties such as color, state, odor, and temperature.
- A **physical change** changes form or appearance but does not create a new substance. Examples: melting ice, tearing paper, boiling water.
- A **chemical change** forms one or more new substances with new properties.
- Some changes are reversible, but reversibility alone is not the only test. The stronger question is: **Did a new substance form?**

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Student-Friendly Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical reaction | A process where atoms are rearranged to form new substances | Vinegar reacting with baking soda |
| Chemical change | A change that produces a new substance | Iron forming rust |
| Physical change | A change in size, shape, or state without making a new substance | Water freezing into ice |
| Reactants | Starting substances in a reaction | Vinegar and baking soda |
| Products | New substances formed in a reaction | Carbon dioxide gas from vinegar and baking soda |
| Precipitate | A solid that forms when two solutions are mixed | A cloudy solid appearing in a clear mixture |
| Indicator | Observable evidence that suggests a reaction may have happened | Color change, gas bubbles, temperature change |

## Main Concept Explanation

### What makes a reaction different?

A chemical reaction happens when starting substances, called **reactants**, change into new substances, called **products**. The products have properties that the reactants did not have.

You cannot usually see atoms rearranging directly, so you look for clues. These clues are called **indicators of a chemical reaction**.

### Five common indicators

| Indicator | What You Observe | Everyday Example | Why It Matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Color change | A new color appears and is not just mixing dyes | Apple slices turn brown; iron turns reddish-brown | New substances may have formed |
| Formation of a precipitate | A solid appears after two liquids are mixed | A cloudy solid forms in a solution | A new insoluble solid may have formed |
| Release of gas | Bubbles, fizzing, or foaming appear without boiling | Vinegar and baking soda fizz | A gas product may have formed |
| Production of odor | A new smell appears | Food spoils; eggs rot | New substances with strong odors may have formed |
| Temperature change | The mixture warms or cools by itself | A heat pack warms; a cold pack cools | Energy may be released or absorbed by a reaction |

### Use evidence carefully

One clue alone is not always proof. For example, boiling water makes bubbles, but those bubbles are water vapor from a physical change. No new substance is formed.

A better test is to ask:

1. What changed?
2. Could the change be explained by a physical process?
3. Is there evidence that a new substance with new properties formed?

### Chemical or physical? A decision guide

| Ask This Question | If Yes | If No |
|---|---|---|
| Did a new substance with new properties form? | Chemical change is likely | Keep checking |
| Was there only a change in state, size, or shape? | Physical change is likely | Keep checking |
| Are there indicators such as gas, precipitate, odor, color, or temperature change? | Chemical reaction may have happened | A reaction may still happen, but evidence is weak |
| Could the clue be caused by boiling, dissolving, crushing, or mixing colors? | Be careful: it may be physical | Chemical explanation becomes stronger |

> [!IMPORTANT] Key Idea Box
>
> The best evidence for a chemical reaction is not just "something changed." The best evidence is that **a new substance with new properties formed**. Indicators are useful clues, but they must be interpreted carefully.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Melting ice

**Observation:** Solid ice becomes liquid water.

**Evidence:** The state changed from solid to liquid. The substance is still water.

**Conclusion:** Physical change.

**Reason:** No new substance formed.

### Example 2: Vinegar plus baking soda

**Observation:** The mixture bubbles and fizzes.

**Evidence:** The bubbles are carbon dioxide gas produced by a reaction. The reactants changed into new products.

**Conclusion:** Chemical change.

**Reason:** A new gas formed.

### Example 3: A cut apple turns brown

**Observation:** The cut surface slowly changes from pale to brown.

**Evidence:** The apple reacts with oxygen in air, producing new brown-colored substances.

**Conclusion:** Chemical change.

**Reason:** The color change is linked to new substances forming.

### Example 4: Boiling water

**Observation:** Bubbles rise from the water.

**Evidence:** The bubbles are water vapor, not a new gas substance.

**Conclusion:** Physical change.

**Reason:** Water changed state from liquid to gas.

## Misconception Alerts

> [!WARNING] Trap 1: "Bubbles always mean a chemical reaction."
>
> Not always. Boiling water bubbles because liquid water becomes water vapor. That is a physical change. Bubbles are stronger evidence when they appear without boiling.

> [!WARNING] Trap 2: "All color changes are chemical."
>
> Not always. Mixing blue and yellow food coloring makes green, but that is just physical mixing. A chemical color change usually means a new substance formed.

> [!WARNING] Trap 3: "If I cannot reverse it, it must be chemical."
>
> Not always. Breaking glass is hard to reverse, but the glass is still glass. Reversibility is helpful, but the key question is whether a new substance formed.

## Error Analysis

Read the student answer, then fix the reasoning.

**Scenario:** Water is heated in a kettle and bubbles appear.

**Student answer:** "This is a chemical reaction because gas bubbles formed."

**What is wrong?** The student noticed a real indicator but did not ask what the gas is. In boiling, the gas is water vapor. It is still H2O, so no new substance formed.

**Better answer:** "This is a physical change because liquid water became water vapor. Bubbles alone are not enough evidence for a chemical reaction."

Now try one:

**Scenario:** Two clear liquids are mixed. The mixture turns cloudy and a white solid settles at the bottom.

**Student answer:** "This is physical because the liquids were only mixed."

**Fix the reasoning:** A solid forming from two liquids is a precipitate. That is evidence that a new substance formed, so a chemical reaction likely happened.

## Final Summary

A chemical reaction forms new substances. Because new substances have different properties, reactions often show visible or sensory clues: color change, precipitate formation, gas release, odor production, or temperature change. These clues are called indicators, but they must be interpreted carefully. Bubbles from boiling water, a color made by mixing dyes, or a shape change from cutting paper may look dramatic but still be physical changes. Always ask: **Did a new substance form?**
