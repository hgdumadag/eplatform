## What You Should Already Know

Before starting, make sure these ideas feel familiar:

- A **population** is the whole group a claim is about.
- A **sample** is the smaller group actually measured or surveyed.
- A percent compares a part to a whole.
- A conclusion should say what the number means in the situation.
- A graph or survey result is stronger when the source, sample size, and labels are clear.

> [!CHECK] Pre-Check
>
> 1. A survey asks 30 Grade 10 learners from one section about all Grade 10 learners. What is the sample?
> 2. What is the population if the claim says, "Most students in the school prefer morning programs"?
> 3. Why might a survey posted only in a gaming club chat give weak evidence about all students?
>
> **Answers:** 1. The 30 learners from one section. 2. All students in the school. 3. The people who see or answer the survey may not reflect the whole school.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Population | The entire group the claim describes |
| Sample | The smaller group from the population that is actually studied |
| Representative sample | A sample that reasonably reflects the important characteristics of the population |
| Bias | A pattern that makes data lean unfairly toward one result |
| Random sample | A sample chosen so each member has a fair chance of being selected |
| Stratified sample | A sample chosen from important subgroups, such as grade levels or sections |
| Convenience sample | A sample chosen because people are easy to reach |
| Voluntary response sample | A sample made from people who choose to respond, often because they feel strongly |

> [!TARGET] Target Skill
>
> Do not ask only, "What percent did the sample show?" Ask, "Can this sample fairly speak for the population in the claim?"

## Visual Introduction: The Sample Must Match the Claim

Representative data begins with a match.

![Population and sample map showing how a smaller group is used to describe a larger group](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-2-representative-data/images/population-sample-map.svg)

If the claim is about **all Grade 10 learners**, the sample should include Grade 10 learners in a fair way. If the claim is about **all students in the school**, a sample from only one club, one section, or one time of day may be too narrow.

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Idea
>
> A sample can be large and still biased. A sample can be small and still useful for a quick check. The question is whether the sample was chosen in a way that fairly matches the population and claim.

## Main Concept 1: Name the Population and Sample

Start every representative-data question with two labels.

| Question to Ask | Example Answer |
|---|---|
| Who is the claim about? | All Grade 10 learners |
| Who was actually surveyed or measured? | 40 learners from the canteen line |
| Does the sample match the claim? | Maybe not; canteen-line learners may not represent learners absent, in clubs, or in other areas |

### Quick Check

A post says, "Students at Mabini High prefer online homework." The data came from 25 students who answered an online poll in the student council group chat.

- **Population:** students at Mabini High
- **Sample:** 25 students who answered the online poll
- **Concern:** voluntary response and online access may bias the result

## Main Concept 2: Look for Bias

Bias is not the same as being wrong on purpose. Bias means the method can push the data toward one kind of answer.

![Comparison between a biased hallway sample and a balanced schoolwide sample](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-2-representative-data/images/biased-vs-representative-sample.svg)

Common warning signs:

- The sample comes from only one easy-to-reach place.
- People choose whether to respond.
- Important subgroups are missing.
- The question is worded to push one answer.
- The conclusion talks about a bigger population than the sample can support.

> [!WARNING] Bias Alert
>
> "I asked my friends" is usually a convenience sample. It may be useful for a personal decision, but it is weak evidence for a claim about an entire class, grade level, or school.

## Main Concept 3: Compare Sampling Methods

Different methods produce different levels of trust.

![Sampling methods grid comparing random, stratified, convenience, and voluntary response samples](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-2-representative-data/images/sampling-methods-grid.svg)

| Method | How It Works | Usually Stronger or Weaker? |
|---|---|---|
| Random sample | Select names using a fair random process | Stronger |
| Stratified sample | Sample from important groups, such as each section | Stronger when groups differ |
| Convenience sample | Ask whoever is easiest to reach | Weaker |
| Voluntary response | Use answers from people who choose to reply | Weaker |

> [!TIP] Fast Test
>
> If the selection method gives every part of the population a fair chance to appear, the evidence is usually stronger.

## Worked Example

**Problem:** A school report claims, "Most Grade 10 learners want a later lunch period." The report is based on a survey of 60 learners waiting in the lunch line at 12:30 p.m. Of those, 42 said they wanted a later lunch.

**Step 1: Identify the population.**  
The claim is about **all Grade 10 learners**.

**Step 2: Identify the sample.**  
The sample is **60 learners waiting in the lunch line at 12:30 p.m.**

**Step 3: Read the statistic.**

$$
\frac{42}{60}=0.70=70\%
$$

In the sample, 70% wanted a later lunch.

**Step 4: Check representativeness.**  
The sample may be biased because it includes only learners already waiting for lunch at one time. It may miss learners eating earlier, bringing food, absent that day, or in different lunch groups.

**Step 5: Write a careful conclusion.**

> [!EXAMPLE] Complete Answer
>
> In the lunch-line sample, 70% of learners wanted a later lunch. However, the data may not represent all Grade 10 learners because the sample came from one place at one time. The report should use a more balanced sample before claiming what most Grade 10 learners want.

## Evidence Checklist

Use this checklist before trusting a claim.

![Bias checklist showing source, size, selection, wording, and match to claim](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-2-representative-data/images/bias-checklist.svg)

| Check | Stronger Evidence Looks Like |
|---|---|
| Source | The data source is named and relevant |
| Size | The sample is large enough for the purpose |
| Selection | People were chosen fairly, not only because they were nearby |
| Wording | Survey questions are neutral |
| Match | The sample matches the population named in the claim |

## Claim Support Scale

Not every data set supports a claim equally well.

![Claim support scale moving from weak to strong evidence](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-2-representative-data/images/claim-support-scale.svg)

| Evidence Level | What It Means |
|---|---|
| Weak | Sample is biased, too narrow, or unclear |
| Limited | Sample gives a clue but has important missing details |
| Reasonable | Sample mostly matches the population and has a clear method |
| Strong | Sample is fair, relevant, large enough, and clearly described |

## Independent Practice

Complete these before checking the answer key.

1. A survey asks 50 students from one Grade 10 section about all Grade 10 learners. Identify the population and sample.
2. A website poll asks, "Do you agree that our amazing school lunch is the best?" What wording problem do you notice?
3. A principal randomly selects 8 students from each Grade 10 section to ask about study spaces. What sampling method is this closest to?
4. A report says 18 out of 30 club officers prefer a new schedule. What percent of the sample preferred the new schedule?
5. Why should the report in item 4 avoid claiming, "Most students prefer the new schedule"?
6. Give one way to improve a convenience sample.
7. Write a careful conclusion for this result: 64 out of 80 randomly selected Grade 10 learners prefer digital reviewers.
8. Name two details that should be included when reporting a survey result.

## Answer Key with Explanations

1. Population: all Grade 10 learners. Sample: 50 students from one Grade 10 section. One section may not reflect every section.
2. The wording is leading because "amazing" and "best" push the respondent toward a positive answer.
3. Stratified sampling, because students are selected from each section.
4. \(\frac{18}{30}=0.60=60\%\).
5. Club officers may not represent all students, so the claim is broader than the sample supports.
6. Sample answer: randomly choose students from multiple sections instead of asking only nearby people.
7. Sample answer: In this random Grade 10 sample, 80% preferred digital reviewers, so the data give reasonable evidence that digital reviewers are popular among Grade 10 learners.
8. Sample answer: sample size, population, selection method, survey question, date, source, or percent result.

## Misconception Alerts

> [!WARNING] Misconception 1: "A bigger sample is always representative."
>
> Size helps, but selection matters. A large sample from only one club can still be biased.

> [!WARNING] Misconception 2: "A sample percent automatically proves a population claim."
>
> The percent describes the sample first. It describes the population only when the sample reasonably represents that population.

> [!WARNING] Misconception 3: "All survey questions are neutral."
>
> Loaded or leading words can influence answers before the data are even collected.

## Error Analysis

A learner writes:

> "I asked 100 followers on my account, and 82% like short videos. Therefore, 82% of all Grade 10 learners like short videos."

**Find the mistake:** The sample is made from the learner's followers, not a fair selection of all Grade 10 learners.

**Correct reasoning:** The statistic can describe the followers who answered. To make a Grade 10 claim, the learner needs a sample chosen fairly from the Grade 10 population.

## Final Summary

Representative data are about fairness between a claim and the evidence used to support it. Always name the population, name the sample, check for bias, and then decide how strong the conclusion can be.
