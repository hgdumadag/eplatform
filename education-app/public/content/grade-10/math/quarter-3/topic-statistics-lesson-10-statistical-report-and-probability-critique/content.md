## What You Should Already Know

Before starting, make sure these ideas feel familiar:

- A statistic describes data from a group.
- A probability is a number from `0` to `1`, or from `0%` to `100%`.
- The denominator tells which total group is being counted.
- A graph needs a title, labels, scale, and source to be interpreted fairly.
- A conclusion should say what the data support, not what someone wishes were true.

### Pre-check / Readiness Quiz

Try these first. Then reveal the answers.

1. In a survey of 40 students, 24 chose basketball. What is the denominator for the probability that a randomly chosen surveyed student chose basketball?
2. A report says "75% improved" but does not say how many students were studied. What key detail is missing?
3. True or false: A graph can be misleading even when all the numbers are correct.
4. A question says "among students who joined a club." Should the denominator be all students or only club members?

<details>
<summary>Reveal pre-check answers</summary>

1. The denominator is `40`, the number of surveyed students.
2. The sample size is missing.
3. True. Scale, labels, category choices, or missing context can make a graph misleading.
4. Only club members. The phrase "among students who joined a club" changes the group being counted.

If these felt difficult, review fractions, percents, graph labels, and basic probability before continuing.
</details>

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Statistical report | A written, visual, or spoken summary that uses data to support a claim |
| Claim | A statement the report wants the reader to believe |
| Evidence | The actual numbers, display, sample, or probability calculation used to support a claim |
| Population | The full group the report is trying to describe |
| Sample | The smaller group actually measured or surveyed |
| Representative sample | A sample that reasonably reflects the population being described |
| Bias | A feature of the sample, question, graph, or wording that can push results in an unfair direction |
| Event | An outcome or set of outcomes in a probability situation |
| Complement | The event that the original event does not happen |
| Conditional probability | A probability calculated after restricting attention to a smaller "given" group |
| Denominator | The total number of possible outcomes or the total group being considered |
| Evidence-based conclusion | A conclusion that names the data result and explains what it means in context |

> [!TARGET] Target Skill
>
> Decide whether a report's claim is supported by the data and whether its probability language matches the event being counted.

## Try Before You Read

A student council report says:

> "Most Grade 10 students prefer a sports day because 30 out of 50 surveyed students chose sports day."

Before doing any new calculation, ask:

1. Who was surveyed?
2. Is `50` a fair sample of all Grade 10 students?
3. Does `30 out of 50` really mean "most"?
4. Does the conclusion describe only the sample, or all Grade 10 students?

<details>
<summary>Reveal thinking guide</summary>

The computation `30/50 = 60%` supports the statement "most surveyed students chose sports day." It may or may not support "most Grade 10 students" unless the sample was representative.
</details>

## Visual Introduction

A strong critique is not just a complaint. It is a careful check of the evidence chain.

![Critique checklist for a statistical report](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-10-statistical-report-and-probability-critique/images/report-critique-checklist.svg)

Use this routine:

1. **Claim:** What is the report saying?
2. **Source:** Where did the data come from?
3. **Sample:** Who was included, and who may have been left out?
4. **Display:** Are the graph, table, labels, and scale fair?
5. **Probability:** Is the denominator or condition correct?
6. **Conclusion:** Does the final statement stay within the evidence?

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Idea
>
> A report can use correct arithmetic and still make an unsupported conclusion. Your job is to check both the calculation and the reasoning.

## Main Concept Explanation

### 1. Separate the Claim from the Evidence

Reports often blend a claim with data:

> "The new study program is effective because 18 of 24 students improved."

The **claim** is that the study program is effective.  
The **evidence** is that `18` out of `24` students improved.

The evidence gives:

`18/24 = 0.75 = 75%`

That supports this careful conclusion:

> In the studied group, 75% of students improved after using the program.

It does not automatically prove:

> The program will improve every student's score.

The second statement goes beyond the evidence.

### 2. Check Whether the Sample Matches the Population

The population is the full group the report talks about. The sample is the group actually measured.

![Survey sample compared with the population](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-10-statistical-report-and-probability-critique/images/sample-population-map.svg)

| Report wording | Population being described | Sample actually measured | Critique question |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Grade 10 students prefer online review." | All Grade 10 students | 25 students from one section | Does one section represent all sections? |
| "Most can finish the task in 10 minutes." | All students doing the task | 12 fastest finishers | Were slower students excluded? |
| "Parents support the schedule." | All parents | 31 parents who replied online | Who did not reply, and could that matter? |

> [!WARNING] Sample Trap
>
> A sample can be large but still biased. Size matters, but fairness of selection matters too.

### 3. Match the Denominator to the Event Language

Probability critique often comes down to one question:

> What is the total group after reading every word in the prompt?

![Probability denominator decision tree](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-10-statistical-report-and-probability-critique/images/probability-denominator-tree.svg)

Use this table from a survey of 60 Grade 10 students:

| Activity choice | Joined a club | Did not join a club | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sports day | 18 | 12 | 30 |
| Arts day | 10 | 20 | 30 |
| Total | 28 | 32 | 60 |

Read the event language carefully:

| Question | Denominator | Probability |
|---|---:|---:|
| Random surveyed student chose sports day | `60` | `30/60 = 1/2` |
| Random surveyed student joined a club | `60` | `28/60 = 7/15` |
| Among club members, chose sports day | `28` | `18/28 = 9/14` |
| Among students who chose arts day, joined a club | `30` | `10/30 = 1/3` |
| Did not choose sports day | `60` | `30/60 = 1/2` |

The word **among** is a strong clue that you are using conditional probability. It restricts the denominator.

### 4. Inspect the Display Before Trusting the Impression

A graph can be technically based on correct data and still create a false visual impression.

![Misleading display with a truncated scale](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-10-statistical-report-and-probability-critique/images/misleading-display-alert.svg)

Common display problems:

- The vertical axis does not start at zero when bar lengths are being compared.
- Labels or units are missing.
- Category names are vague.
- Percentages are shown without sample size.
- A pie chart or stacked bar uses categories that overlap.
- The title makes a stronger claim than the data show.

> [!CHECK] Display Check
>
> Before accepting the message of a graph, read the title, labels, scale, source, sample size, and category definitions.

### 5. Write a Conclusion That Fits the Evidence

Use this writing frame:

![Evidence-to-conclusion writing frame](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-10-statistical-report-and-probability-critique/images/evidence-conclusion-frame.svg)

| Part | Sentence starter |
|---|---|
| Evidence | "The data show..." |
| Group | "In this sample..." or "Among..." |
| Calculation | "The probability is..." or "The percent is..." |
| Caution | "However, this does not prove..." |
| Conclusion | "A fair conclusion is..." |

Good critique language sounds precise:

- "The claim is supported for the surveyed students, but not necessarily for all Grade 10 students."
- "The probability should use `28` as the denominator because the question says among club members."
- "The graph exaggerates the difference because the axis begins at `40%` instead of `0%`."

## Rule Box

Use the **R.E.P.O.R.T.** routine:

| Letter | Question to ask |
|---|---|
| R - Read the claim | What is the report trying to prove? |
| E - Examine the source | Where did the data come from? |
| P - Pin down the population and sample | Who is described, and who was measured? |
| O - Observe the display | Are labels, scales, categories, and sample size clear? |
| R - Recalculate if needed | Is the percent or probability computed with the correct denominator? |
| T - Tie conclusion to evidence | What can be fairly said from the data? |

## Worked Example

### Example: Critique a School Activity Report

**Report:** "Sports day should replace arts day because most Grade 10 students prefer sports day. In a survey, 30 of 60 students selected sports day. Also, among students who joined a club, 18 selected sports day."

Use the survey table:

| Activity choice | Joined a club | Did not join a club | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Sports day | 18 | 12 | 30 |
| Arts day | 10 | 20 | 30 |
| Total | 28 | 32 | 60 |

**Question 1:** Is the statement "most surveyed students prefer sports day" supported?

**Step 1: Identify the denominator.**

The statement is about all surveyed students, so the denominator is `60`.

**Step 2: Calculate.**

`30/60 = 1/2 = 50%`

**Step 3: Interpret.**

`50%` is exactly half, not more than half.

**Answer:** No. The data show that half of the surveyed students selected sports day, so "most surveyed students" is too strong.

**Question 2:** What is the probability that a surveyed club member selected sports day?

**Step 1: Read the condition.**

The phrase "club member" means the denominator is the number of club members, `28`.

**Step 2: Count favorable outcomes.**

There are `18` club members who selected sports day.

**Step 3: Calculate.**

`P(sports day | club member) = 18/28 = 9/14`

**Step 4: Write a conclusion.**

Among surveyed students who joined a club, the probability of selecting sports day is `9/14`, or about `64%`.

> [!EXAMPLE] Complete Critique
>
> The probability claim about club members is calculated correctly if it uses `28` as the denominator. However, the broader claim that "most Grade 10 students prefer sports day" is not supported by this table because only `30/60`, or `50%`, of surveyed students selected sports day.

## Guided Practice

### Guided Problem 1

A report says, "Most surveyed students use printed reviewers." The table shows:

| Reviewer type | Students |
|---|---:|
| Printed reviewer | 22 |
| Online reviewer | 18 |
| No reviewer | 10 |
| Total | 50 |

Is the word "most" fair?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

Use all surveyed students as the denominator.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Compute `22/50`.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

`22/50 = 44%`. The word "most" usually means more than half. Since `44%` is less than half, the statement is not fair. A better conclusion is: "Printed reviewers were the most common single choice, but fewer than half of surveyed students chose them."
</details>

### Guided Problem 2

In a survey of 80 students, 48 joined an academic club. Of the club members, 30 attended a Saturday review session.

What is the probability that a randomly selected club member attended the Saturday review?

<details>
<summary>Hint 1</summary>

The phrase "club member" restricts the denominator.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Hint 2</summary>

Use `30/48`, not `30/80`.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

`P(attended review | club member) = 30/48 = 5/8 = 62.5%`.

Among students who joined an academic club, `5/8` attended the Saturday review.
</details>

### Guided Problem 3

A bar graph compares two sections' quiz pass rates:

- Section A: `76%`
- Section B: `80%`

The graph's vertical axis begins at `70%`, so Section B's bar looks much taller.

What should your critique mention?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the real numerical difference with the visual impression.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

The real difference is only `4` percentage points. Starting the axis at `70%` exaggerates the visual difference. The graph should use a clearer scale or the report should state the small difference directly.
</details>

1. The population is the full group being described; the sample is the smaller group actually measured.
2. "Among" restricts the group to a condition, so the denominator becomes that smaller group.

## Independent Practice

Complete these before checking the answer key.

Use this table for Items 1-5.

| Lunch choice | Male students | Female students | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Rice meal | 14 | 18 | 32 |
| Sandwich | 10 | 8 | 18 |
| Total | 24 | 26 | 50 |

1. What is the probability that a randomly selected surveyed student chose rice meal?
2. What is the probability that a randomly selected male student chose sandwich?
3. A report says, "Most surveyed students chose rice meal." Is this supported? Explain.
4. A report says, "Among female students, rice meal was chosen by 18 out of 26." Is the denominator correct? Explain.
5. What is the complement of choosing rice meal in this table?

Use the short report below for Items 6-8.

> A class survey asked 15 student athletes whether they preferred morning or afternoon practice. Ten chose afternoon practice. The headline says, "Grade 10 students strongly prefer afternoon practice."

6. What is one reason the headline may be too broad?
7. What percent of the surveyed student athletes chose afternoon practice?
8. Write a fairer conclusion.

## Answer Key with Explanations

1. `32/50 = 16/25 = 64%`. The denominator is all `50` surveyed students.
2. `10/24 = 5/12`. The phrase "male student" restricts the denominator to the `24` male students.
3. Yes, for surveyed students. `32/50 = 64%`, which is more than half. It should not automatically be generalized beyond the surveyed group.
4. Yes. "Among female students" means the denominator is the total number of female students, `26`.
5. The complement is choosing sandwich. Its probability is `18/50 = 9/25`.
6. The sample includes only `15` student athletes, not all Grade 10 students.
7. `10/15 = 2/3`, or about `66.7%`.
8. Sample: "In this survey of 15 student athletes, about two-thirds preferred afternoon practice."

## Misconception Alerts

> [!WARNING] Misconception 1: "Most" means the largest category.
>
> Not always. A category can be the largest single category without being more than half of the total.

> [!WARNING] Misconception 2: The denominator is always the grand total.
>
> Conditional probability uses the group after the condition. If the question says "among club members," use the number of club members.

> [!WARNING] Misconception 3: A neat graph is automatically trustworthy.
>
> A graph can look professional while still hiding sample size, using a misleading scale, or making an exaggerated title claim.

> [!WARNING] Misconception 4: A sample result proves the same result for everyone.
>
> Sample data can suggest a pattern, but generalizing to a population requires a representative sample and careful wording.

## Error Analysis

A student writes:

> "The report is true because `18/60 = 30%`, so many club members chose sports day."

**Find the mistake:** The student used `60`, the total number of surveyed students, even though the phrase "club members" restricts the denominator.

**Correct reasoning:** There are `28` club members, and `18` of them chose sports day. The correct probability is `18/28 = 9/14`, about `64%`.

**Better conclusion:** Among surveyed club members, about `64%` chose sports day. That supports a statement about surveyed club members, not automatically all Grade 10 students.

## Final Summary

Statistical report critique is careful reading plus careful math. First identify the claim, source, sample, display, and event language. Then calculate with the correct denominator. Finally, write a conclusion that says exactly what the data support, with no exaggeration.
