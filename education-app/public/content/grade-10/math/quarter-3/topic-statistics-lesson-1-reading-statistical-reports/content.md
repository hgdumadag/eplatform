## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Ask yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Claim | The statement the report wants the reader to believe | What is the report saying is true? |
| Data source | Where the data came from, such as a survey, records, test results, or observation | Who or what was measured? |
| Display | A table, graph, chart, or diagram used to show the data | What does the visual show, and what might it hide? |
| Statistic | A number that summarizes data, such as a percent, mean, median, count, or ratio | What number is being used as evidence? |
| Sample | The smaller group measured to learn about a larger group | Does this group match the population in the claim? |
| Conclusion | The final interpretation based on the evidence | Does the conclusion stay within what the data can support? |

> [!IMPORTANT] Core Idea
>
> A statistical report is not just a number. Read the **claim**, **source**, **display**, **statistic**, and **conclusion** together.

## Visual Introduction: Read the Report Like a Detective

![Anatomy of a statistical report](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-1-reading-statistical-reports/images/report-anatomy.svg)

A school newsletter says:

> "Most Grade 10 learners prefer video lessons. In a survey of 50 learners, 32 chose video lessons as their preferred review format."

Before agreeing, separate the parts:

| Report part | In the newsletter example |
|---|---|
| Claim | Most Grade 10 learners prefer video lessons |
| Data source | Survey of 50 learners |
| Statistic | 32 out of 50, or 64% |
| Display | The newsletter might use a bar graph or table |
| Conclusion check | The claim is reasonable for the surveyed group; it may need a representative sample to describe all Grade 10 learners |

## 1. Identify the Claim First

The claim is the main statement being supported. It often uses words like **most**, **more than**, **less than**, **typical**, **increased**, **decreased**, or **preferred**.

### Example 1: Finding the Claim

**Report:** "After a school review program, the average quiz score increased from 21 to 26 points. The report says the program improved performance."

**Claim:** The program improved performance.

**Evidence:** The average score increased from 21 to 26.

**Careful reading:** The evidence supports improvement for the group measured, but we still need to know who joined the program and whether anything else changed.

> [!TIP] Fast Move
>
> Underline the sentence the report wants you to believe. That sentence is usually the claim.

## 2. Trace the Data Source

![Path from data source to claim](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-1-reading-statistical-reports/images/source-to-claim.svg)

The data source tells where the evidence came from. A strong report names the group, sample size, time period, and method.

| Source detail | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Group measured | Shows who the statistic describes |
| Sample size | Helps judge how much evidence was collected |
| Method | Reveals possible bias, such as voluntary response |
| Time period | Shows whether the data are current for the claim |

### Example 2: Source Check

**Report:** "80% of students want longer lunch breaks."

**Source note:** The survey was posted online and 20 students answered.

**Reading:** The statistic is clear, but the source is weak for a school-wide claim. The learners who chose to answer may not represent all students.

## 3. Read the Display, Not Just the Caption

![Bar chart reading checklist](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-1-reading-statistical-reports/images/bar-chart-check.svg)

When a report uses a table or graph, check:

1. title
2. axis labels or column labels
3. units
4. scale
5. categories
6. source or note

### Worked Example: Does the Graph Support the Claim?

**Report claim:** "Videos are clearly the most preferred review format."

| Review format | Learners |
|---|---:|
| Video lessons | 32 |
| Printed modules | 11 |
| Group study | 7 |

**Step 1: Identify the statistic.**  
Video lessons were chosen by 32 learners out of 50.

**Step 2: Compute the percent.**

$$
\frac{32}{50}=0.64=64\%
$$

**Step 3: Connect the number to the claim.**  
Since 64% is more than half, "most" is supported for the surveyed learners.

**Step 4: Limit the conclusion to the evidence.**  
The report can say that most surveyed learners preferred video lessons. To describe all Grade 10 learners, we need to know whether the 50 learners were selected fairly.

> [!EXAMPLE] Complete Answer
>
> The data show that 32 of 50 surveyed learners, or 64%, chose video lessons. This supports the claim for the surveyed group, but a school-wide claim needs a representative sample.

## 4. Watch for Misleading Scales and Missing Labels

![Misleading scale comparison](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-1-reading-statistical-reports/images/misleading-scale.svg)

Two graphs can show the same data but create different impressions. A graph can look dramatic if the vertical scale starts near the data values instead of at 0.

| Month | Learners attending review |
|---|---:|
| August | 42 |
| September | 45 |

The increase is real:

$$
45-42=3
$$

But the conclusion should stay measured:

**Careful conclusion:** Attendance increased by 3 learners from August to September.

**Too strong:** Attendance exploded.

> [!WARNING] Common Trap
>
> A tall bar does not automatically mean a large difference. Read the scale and compare the actual numbers.

## 5. Build an Evidence-Based Conclusion

![Evidence-based conclusion triangle](/content/grade-10/math/quarter-3/topic-statistics-lesson-1-reading-statistical-reports/images/conclusion-triangle.svg)

Use this sentence frame:

> The data show **[statistic]** for **[group/source]**, so **[claim]** is **supported / partly supported / not supported** because **[reason]**.

### Example 3: Conclusion Frame

**Report:** "In a survey of 80 Grade 10 learners, 52 said they study better with practice quizzes. The report claims most surveyed learners prefer quizzes for review."

**Statistic:**

$$
\frac{52}{80}=0.65=65\%
$$

**Conclusion:** The data show that 65% of the surveyed Grade 10 learners prefer practice quizzes, so the claim is supported for the surveyed group because more than half chose quizzes.

## Guided Practice

### Guided Problem 1

**Report:** "A survey of 40 learners found that 28 prefer studying with classmates. The report says most surveyed learners prefer group study."

What is the statistic as a percent?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Divide the number who prefer group study by the total surveyed.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

\(\frac{28}{40}=0.70=70\%\). The statistic is 70%.
</details>

### Guided Problem 2

**Report:** "Students are unhappy with the cafeteria." The source says only 12 students from one club answered the survey.

What should you question before accepting the claim?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Compare the sample to the population named in the claim.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

Ask whether 12 students from one club represent all students. The sample may be too small or biased.
</details>

### Guided Problem 3

A graph compares 78% and 80%, but the vertical axis starts at 75%.

Why could this graph be misleading?

<details>
<summary>Hint</summary>

Think about how the bars would look if the scale started at 0%.
</details>

<details>
<summary>Show solution</summary>

The difference is only 2 percentage points, but starting the axis at 75% can make the bars look much farther apart than they are.
</details>

## Independent Practice

Complete these before checking the answer key.

1. A report says "Most students use the library weekly." Identify the claim.
2. A survey asked 25 learners, and 15 said yes. Find the percent who said yes.
3. Name two source details a report should include.
4. A bar graph has no axis label. Explain why that is a problem.
5. A report about all Grade 10 learners uses data from one section only. What concern should you raise?
6. Write a careful conclusion for this result: 48 out of 80 surveyed learners prefer printed modules.
7. A graph shows 51% and 54% with a cut-off scale. What exact difference should the report mention?
8. Create one question you would ask before accepting a statistical claim from social media.

## Answer Key with Explanations

1. The claim is that most students use the library weekly. It is the statement the report wants readers to believe.
2. \(15 \div 25 = 0.60 = 60\%\). The statistic describes the surveyed learners.
3. Sample answers: group measured, sample size, method, location, time period, or source organization.
4. Without an axis label, readers may not know whether the values are counts, percents, pesos, scores, or another unit.
5. The sample may not represent all Grade 10 learners because one section may have different habits from the whole grade level.
6. Sample: The data show that 48 of 80 surveyed learners, or 60%, prefer printed modules, so the claim "most surveyed learners prefer printed modules" is supported for that group.
7. The exact difference is 3 percentage points. A cut-off scale may exaggerate the visual difference.
8. Sample: Who collected the data, how many people were included, and how were they chosen?

## Misconception Alerts

> [!WARNING] Misconception 1: "A percent proves the claim."
>
> A percent is useful only when you know the group, sample size, and question behind it.

> [!WARNING] Misconception 2: "A graph is automatically objective."
>
> Graphs are designed by people. Scales, labels, categories, and omitted data can change the reader's impression.

> [!WARNING] Misconception 3: "The conclusion can describe everyone."
>
> A conclusion should describe only the population supported by the data. If the sample is limited, the conclusion must be limited too.

## Error Analysis

A student writes:

> "The graph shows 70%, so all Grade 10 learners prefer online review."

**Find the mistake:** The student uses the statistic but ignores the source and population. We do not know who was surveyed or whether they represent all Grade 10 learners.

**Correct reasoning:** State the statistic and the group it describes. Then decide whether the source is strong enough for the broader claim.

## Self-Explanation Prompts

Answer these in your own words.

1. Why should the claim be identified before the statistic?
2. How can a data source make a report stronger or weaker?
3. What should you check before trusting a graph?
4. How do you write a conclusion that does not exaggerate the evidence?

**Sample Responses**

1. The claim tells me what the statistic is supposed to support.
2. A clear, fair source makes the evidence stronger; a small or biased source weakens the claim.
3. I should check the title, labels, units, scale, categories, and source.
4. I should name the statistic, name the group measured, and avoid describing a larger group unless the data support it.

## Mastery Checklist

You are ready for practice when you can:

- identify the claim in a report;
- name the data source and sample size when given;
- read a table or graph using labels, units, and scale;
- compute a simple percent from a count and total;
- decide whether a statistic supports, partly supports, or does not support a claim;
- write a conclusion that stays within the evidence.

> [!PRACTICE] What To Do Next
>
> Complete the practice exam first for quick feedback on claims, sources, displays, and statistics. Then take the assessment when you can explain whether a report's conclusion is supported by its evidence.

## Final Summary

Reading statistical reports means slowing down before agreeing with a claim. Identify what the report says, where the data came from, how the data are displayed, which statistic is used, and whether the conclusion matches the evidence.
