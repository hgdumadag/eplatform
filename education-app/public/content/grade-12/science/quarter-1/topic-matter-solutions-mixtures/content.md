# Matter, Solutions, and Mixtures

## Introduction

Understanding the classification and properties of matter is fundamental to chemistry and essential for the UPCAT. Matter can be classified as either a **pure substance** or a **mixture**, and within mixtures, we distinguish between solutions, colloids, and suspensions based on particle size and observable properties. This topic explores how to identify these categories, understand their distinguishing characteristics, and perform quantitative calculations for density and molality—skills critical for success in advanced chemistry problems on standardized exams.

In this module, we will examine how pure substances differ from mixtures, explore the Tyndall effect as a diagnostic tool for distinguishing colloids from solutions, calculate density using fundamental formulas, and apply molality calculations to concentration problems. Mastery of these concepts will enable you to solve complex UPCAT problems involving mixture separations, density-based predictions, and solution stoichiometry.

## Classification of Matter

Matter can be divided into two broad categories: **pure substances** and **mixtures**.

### Pure Substances

A **pure substance** has a fixed composition and fixed properties (melting point, boiling point, density). Pure substances are further subdivided into **elements** and **compounds**.

**Elements**: A pure substance consisting of only one type of atom. Elements are represented by a one- or two-letter symbol (e.g., O, Fe, Cl). At the atomic level, an element like oxygen exists as O₂ (diatomic), or in the case of noble gases like He, as individual atoms. Elements cannot be broken down into simpler substances by ordinary chemical means.

**Compounds**: A pure substance formed when two or more elements combine chemically in fixed proportions. Compounds have properties that differ from their constituent elements. For example:
- Water (H₂O): hydrogen and oxygen bonded in a 2:1 ratio
- Sodium chloride (NaCl): sodium and chlorine bonded in a 1:1 ratio
- Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆): carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen bonded together

The chemical composition of a compound is always the same—every sample of pure water contains hydrogen and oxygen in the same ratio.

### Mixtures

A **mixture** is a physical combination of two or more pure substances that retain their individual identities and properties. Unlike compounds, the components of a mixture are not chemically bonded and can be present in variable proportions. Mixtures are classified as either **homogeneous** or **heterogeneous**.

**Homogeneous mixtures** have a uniform composition throughout. You cannot visually distinguish the individual components under normal observation. Examples include saltwater, sugar solution, and air. All solutions are homogeneous mixtures.

**Heterogeneous mixtures** have a non-uniform composition, and you can visually distinguish the different components. Examples include sand and gravel, oil and water, and blood. Suspensions are heterogeneous mixtures.

## Types of Mixtures: Solutions, Colloids, and Suspensions

### Solutions

A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture composed of a solute (the substance dissolved) and a solvent (the substance that does the dissolving, typically present in greater quantity). In aqueous solutions, water is the solvent.

**Characteristics of solutions:**
- Transparent or translucent
- Particles are extremely small: diameter < 1 nm (1 nanometer = 10⁻⁹ m)
- Do not settle over time
- Particles pass through filter paper (cannot be filtered out)
- Do not scatter light (no Tyndall effect visible)

**Examples of solutions:**
- Saltwater (NaCl dissolved in H₂O)
- Sugar solution (sucrose dissolved in H₂O)
- Air (mixture of N₂, O₂, and other gases)
- Brass (copper and zinc alloy)

In a true solution, the solute is dissolved at the molecular or ionic level, meaning individual molecules or ions are dispersed throughout the solvent. This is why solutions appear transparent and uniform.

### Colloids

A **colloid** is a mixture with particle size intermediate between solutions and suspensions. The particles are larger than molecular scale but still too small to be seen individually without a microscope.

**Characteristics of colloids:**
- Particle diameter: 1 nm to 1000 nm (1 μm = 10⁻⁶ m)
- Do not settle under normal gravity over reasonable time scales
- Cannot be separated by ordinary filtration
- Exhibit the **Tyndall effect**: scatter light, making the path of light visible through the mixture
- Appear opaque or cloudy

**The Tyndall Effect**: When a beam of light passes through a colloid, the colloidal particles scatter the light, making the beam visible. This occurs because the particles are large enough to interact with light waves. In contrast, true solutions do not scatter light noticeably because the dissolved particles are too small.

**Examples of colloids:**
- Milk: fat globules dispersed in water
- Fog: water droplets dispersed in air
- Gelatin: protein particles dispersed in water
- Paint: pigment particles dispersed in a liquid medium
- Smoke: solid particles dispersed in air

Colloids are colloquially stable but can sometimes separate over extended periods (e.g., cream separating from milk).

### Suspensions

A **suspension** is a heterogeneous mixture in which relatively large, visible particles are dispersed throughout a medium (usually a liquid).

**Characteristics of suspensions:**
- Particle diameter: > 1000 nm (> 1 μm), visible to the naked eye or with a simple microscope
- Heterogeneous appearance; components are visually distinguishable
- Particles settle over time due to gravity
- Can be separated by filtration
- Do not exhibit the Tyndall effect noticeably (particles are too large and opaque)

**Examples of suspensions:**
- Muddy water: soil particles suspended in water
- Blood: red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets suspended in plasma
- Chalk dust suspended in air
- Flour mixed with water

In suspensions, the particles remain dispersed only while actively stirred or mixed. Gravity causes them to settle to the bottom over time.

### Distinguishing Between Solutions, Colloids, and Suspensions

| Property | Solution | Colloid | Suspension |
|----------|----------|---------|-----------|
| Particle size | < 1 nm | 1 nm – 1 μm | > 1 μm |
| Appearance | Transparent | Opaque/translucent | Opaque, visibly separating |
| Tyndall effect | No | Yes (visible) | Not typical (particles too large) |
| Settles | No | No (or very slowly) | Yes, within hours/days |
| Filterable | No | No | Yes |
| Homogeneous/Heterogeneous | Homogeneous | Homogeneous (appears) | Heterogeneous |
| Separation method | Distillation, evaporation | Centrifugation, dialysis | Filtration |

## Density Calculations

**Density** is the mass of a substance per unit volume. It is an intensive property, meaning it does not depend on the amount of substance.

### Density Formula

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

Where:
- $\rho$ (rho) = density
- $m$ = mass (in grams or kilograms)
- $V$ = volume (in mL, cm³, or L)

### Units of Density

Common units include:
- g/mL (most common for liquids and solids)
- g/cm³ (equivalent to g/mL; 1 cm³ = 1 mL)
- kg/m³ (SI unit)
- g/L (for gases)

**Conversion note**: 1 g/mL = 1000 kg/m³

### Worked Example: Calculating Density

A liquid has a mass of 45 g and occupies a volume of 30 mL. Calculate its density.

**Solution:**
$$\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{45 \text{ g}}{30 \text{ mL}} = 1.5 \text{ g/mL}$$

The density is 1.5 g/mL. This density is characteristic of glycerin.

### Density and Floating/Sinking

Whether an object floats or sinks in a liquid depends on the relative densities:

- **If object density < liquid density**: the object floats
- **If object density > liquid density**: the object sinks
- **If object density = liquid density**: the object is neutrally buoyant

For example, ice (density ≈ 0.92 g/mL) floats in water (density = 1.00 g/mL), while aluminum (density ≈ 2.70 g/mL) sinks in water.

### Reverse Density Calculations

You can also rearrange the density formula to find mass or volume:

$$m = \rho \times V$$
$$V = \frac{m}{\rho}$$

**Example**: What is the volume of 50 g of ethanol if its density is 0.789 g/mL?

$$V = \frac{m}{\rho} = \frac{50 \text{ g}}{0.789 \text{ g/mL}} = 63.4 \text{ mL}$$

## Concentration and Molality

### Molality (m)

**Molality** is a concentration measure that expresses the number of moles of solute per kilogram of solvent (not solution—this is critical).

$$m = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{\text{kg}_{\text{solvent}}}$$

Where:
- $m$ = molality (mol/kg or simply "m")
- $n_{\text{solute}}$ = moles of solute
- $\text{kg}_{\text{solvent}}$ = mass of solvent in kilograms

**Why molality instead of molarity?** Molality is independent of temperature, making it useful in calculations involving colligative properties (freezing point depression, boiling point elevation). Molarity, which is moles per liter of solution, changes with temperature as solutions expand or contract.

### Calculating Molality: Step-by-Step

1. **Find moles of solute**: Use the formula $n = \frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{M}$, where $M$ is the molar mass
2. **Convert mass of solvent to kg**: Divide the mass in grams by 1000
3. **Divide moles by kg of solvent**

### Worked Example: Molality Calculation

10 g of NaOH (molar mass = 40 g/mol) is dissolved in 500 g of water. Calculate the molality.

**Solution:**

Step 1: Calculate moles of NaOH
$$n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{10 \text{ g}}{40 \text{ g/mol}} = 0.25 \text{ mol}$$

Step 2: Convert mass of water to kg
$$500 \text{ g} = 0.5 \text{ kg}$$

Step 3: Calculate molality
$$m = \frac{n}{kg_{\text{solvent}}} = \frac{0.25 \text{ mol}}{0.5 \text{ kg}} = 0.5 \text{ m}$$

The molality is 0.5 m (read as "0.5 molal").

### Molality vs. Molarity

- **Molality (m)**: moles of solute per kg of **solvent** — temperature independent
- **Molarity (M)**: moles of solute per liter of **solution** — temperature dependent

For example, a 1 m NaOH solution dissolves 1 mole of NaOH in 1 kg of water. The resulting solution may occupy slightly more than 1 liter of total volume (depending on the solute and temperature).

## Properties and Separation of Mixtures

### Physical Properties

The identification and separation of mixtures rely on **physical properties**—characteristics that can be observed or measured without changing the chemical composition:
- **Color**: visual observation
- **Density**: mass per unit volume
- **Boiling point**: temperature at which liquid becomes gas
- **Melting point**: temperature at which solid becomes liquid
- **Solubility**: how much solute dissolves in a solvent at a given temperature
- **Hardness**: resistance to deformation

### Separation Techniques

Different mixture types require different separation methods:

**Filtration**: Separates solid particles from a liquid or gas. Used for suspensions. The filter paper allows the solvent to pass but traps larger particles.

**Distillation**: Separates liquid components of a solution based on differences in boiling point. The solution is heated; the component with the lower boiling point vaporizes first, condenses, and is collected separately.

**Chromatography**: Separates components based on their affinity for a mobile phase and stationary phase. Used for solutions of dyes, pigments, or other dissolved substances.

**Crystallization**: Cooling a solution causes solute to precipitate as crystals, allowing separation from the solvent.

**Evaporation**: Heating a solution causes the solvent to evaporate, leaving the solute behind (useful for retrieving a dissolved salt from saltwater).

## Key Formulas

$$\text{Density: } \rho = \frac{m}{V}$$

$$\text{Molality: } m = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{\text{kg}_{\text{solvent}}}$$

$$\text{Moles: } n = \frac{m_{\text{solute}}}{M}$$

## Worked Examples for UPCAT

### Example 1: Classification of Matter

Classify each of the following as a pure substance (element or compound) or a mixture (solution, colloid, or suspension):

a) Oxygen gas (O₂)
b) Saltwater
c) Milk
d) Muddy river water
e) Iron (Fe)

**Solution:**

a) **Oxygen gas (O₂)**: Pure substance (element). It consists only of oxygen atoms bonded together.

b) **Saltwater**: Mixture (solution). It is a homogeneous mixture of salt (solute) dissolved in water (solvent), with particles < 1 nm.

c) **Milk**: Mixture (colloid). It is an opaque mixture of fat globules dispersed in water. The fat particles are 1 nm to 1 μm in diameter and exhibit the Tyndall effect.

d) **Muddy river water**: Mixture (suspension). It is a heterogeneous mixture of soil particles (> 1 μm) suspended in water. The particles settle over time and can be filtered out.

e) **Iron (Fe)**: Pure substance (element). It is a single element consisting only of iron atoms.

### Example 2: Density Calculation

A 25 mL sample of an unknown liquid has a mass of 19.75 g. Calculate its density and identify the liquid.

**Solution:**

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{19.75 \text{ g}}{25 \text{ mL}} = 0.79 \text{ g/mL}$$

The density is 0.79 g/mL, which matches ethanol. The unknown liquid is ethanol (drinking alcohol).

### Example 3: Distinguishing Solutions and Colloids

You have two clear, colorless liquids. You shine a laser pointer through each. Liquid A shows a bright red beam, while Liquid B does not. What can you conclude?

**Solution:**

Liquid A displays the **Tyndall effect** (the beam is visible due to light scattering). This indicates Liquid A is a **colloid**.

Liquid B does not display the Tyndall effect, indicating it is a **solution** or pure liquid.

The colloidal particles in Liquid A are large enough (1 nm to 1 μm) to scatter light, while the dissolved particles in the solution (Liquid B) are too small.

### Example 4: Molality Calculation

Calculate the molality of a solution prepared by dissolving 5 g of glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆, molar mass = 180 g/mol) in 250 g of water.

**Solution:**

Step 1: Calculate moles of glucose
$$n = \frac{5 \text{ g}}{180 \text{ g/mol}} = 0.0278 \text{ mol}$$

Step 2: Convert mass of water to kg
$$250 \text{ g} = 0.250 \text{ kg}$$

Step 3: Calculate molality
$$m = \frac{0.0278 \text{ mol}}{0.250 \text{ kg}} = 0.111 \text{ m}$$

The molality is 0.111 m or approximately 0.11 m.

### Example 5: Identifying Colloids in Biological Systems

Which of the following is NOT a solution?

a) Seawater
b) Vinegar
c) Blood
d) Bronze

**Solution:**

The answer is **(c) Blood**.

**Seawater** is a solution containing dissolved salts and gases.

**Vinegar** is a solution containing acetic acid and water.

**Blood** is a colloid (and heterogeneous mixture) containing red blood cells, white blood cells, platelets, and proteins dispersed in plasma. It exhibits the Tyndall effect and displays colloidal properties.

**Bronze** is a homogeneous alloy solution of copper and tin (uniform composition).

## UPCAT Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing molality with molarity**: Remember—molality is moles per kilogram of **solvent**, not solution. This distinction is critical for colligative property calculations and is a favorite UPCAT pitfall.

2. **Assuming all clear liquids are solutions**: A clear, transparent liquid could be a solution or a pure liquid. Use the Tyndall effect test to distinguish colloids from solutions. If you can see the laser beam path, it's likely a colloid.

3. **Misidentifying settling behavior**: Solutions never settle; colloids take very long to settle (if at all); suspensions settle within hours to days. If particles separate rapidly, it's a suspension.

4. **Density units**: Always check units. Convert to the same unit system before calculating. For example, ensure mass is in grams if volume is in mL.

5. **Forgetting to convert grams to kilograms in molality**: When calculating molality, the mass of solvent must be in kilograms. A common error is dividing by grams directly.

6. **Assuming pure substances are always single atoms**: Elements can be diatomic (O₂, N₂) or polyatomic. Compounds always consist of two or more different elements bonded together.

7. **Overcomplicating filtration**: Remember, filtration works only for suspensions. Solutions and colloids pass through filter paper because their particles are too small.

## Key Takeaways

- **Matter classification**: Pure substances (elements and compounds) vs. mixtures (homogeneous and heterogeneous)
- **Three main mixture types**: Solutions are transparent with particle size < 1 nm; colloids are opaque and exhibit the Tyndall effect with particle size 1 nm to 1 μm; suspensions have visible particles > 1 μm that settle
- **Density formula**: $\rho = \frac{m}{V}$; use it to identify substances and predict whether objects float or sink
- **Molality**: $m = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{\text{kg}_{\text{solvent}}}$; independent of temperature, essential for colligative property problems
- **Separation techniques**: Choose filtration for suspensions, distillation for liquid mixtures, chromatography for dissolved substances
