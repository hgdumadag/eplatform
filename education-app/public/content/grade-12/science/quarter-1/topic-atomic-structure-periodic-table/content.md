# Atomic Structure and the Periodic Table

## Introduction

The atom is the fundamental unit of matter, and understanding its internal structure is crucial for comprehending chemical behavior, bonding, and reactivity. This module explores the subatomic particles that compose atoms, the organization of electrons in orbitals, and how atomic structure determines the properties of elements as organized in the Periodic Table. For UPCAT success, mastery of these concepts is essential, particularly the ability to calculate atomic number and mass number, determine electron configurations including exceptions due to orbital stability, and recognize periodic trends that govern element chemistry.

We will examine how protons, neutrons, and electrons are arranged; how isotopes differ while remaining the same element; how electrons fill orbitals according to the Aufbau principle, Pauli exclusion principle, and Hund's rule; and how periodic trends like electronegativity, atomic radius, and ionization energy reflect the underlying atomic structure. Pay special attention to half-filled and fully-filled orbital configurations—these exceptions are UPCAT favorites.

## Subatomic Particles

### The Three Fundamental Particles

All atoms consist of three types of subatomic particles:

**Protons:**
- Charge: +1 (positive)
- Mass: 1 atomic mass unit (amu) = 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg
- Location: nucleus (center of atom)
- Define the identity of an element: the **atomic number (Z) equals the number of protons**
- Number of protons = number of electrons in a neutral atom

**Neutrons:**
- Charge: 0 (neutral)
- Mass: approximately 1 amu (slightly more than protons)
- Location: nucleus (alongside protons)
- Do not define the element but affect atomic mass and stability
- Contribute to nuclear stability (N/P ratio)

**Electrons:**
- Charge: -1 (negative)
- Mass: 1/1836 amu; negligible compared to protons and neutrons
- Location: electron cloud (orbitals) surrounding the nucleus
- Responsible for chemical bonding and reactivity
- In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons

### Atomic Number and Mass Number

**Atomic Number (Z):** The number of protons in an atom. The atomic number uniquely identifies an element and determines its position on the periodic table.

**Mass Number (A):** The total number of protons and neutrons in an atom's nucleus.

$$A = Z + N$$

Where:
- $A$ = mass number
- $Z$ = atomic number (protons)
- $N$ = number of neutrons

**Calculating neutrons:** $N = A - Z$

### Worked Example: Identifying Neutrons

Element X has atomic number 8 and mass number 16. How many neutrons does it have?

$$N = A - Z = 16 - 8 = 8 \text{ neutrons}$$

This is oxygen-16, which contains 8 protons and 8 neutrons. The atomic number 8 identifies it as oxygen (O), regardless of the mass number.

## Isotopes

### Definition and Characteristics

**Isotopes** are atoms of the same element (same atomic number Z) that have different numbers of neutrons (different mass number A). Because they have the same number of protons, isotopes have nearly identical chemical properties but different masses and nuclear stability.

### Isotope Notation

Isotopes are denoted in two ways:

1. **Nuclear notation:** $^{A}_{Z}X$ where X is the element symbol, A is mass number (superscript), and Z is atomic number (subscript). Example: $^{14}_{6}C$

2. **Hyphenated notation:** Element-A. Example: Carbon-14 or C-14

For clarity, we typically omit the atomic number in the subscript because it is already determined by the element symbol.

### Examples of Important Isotopes

**Carbon isotopes:**
- Carbon-12 ($^{12}_{6}C$): 6 protons, 6 neutrons. Most abundant (98.9%). **Standard for atomic mass unit**
- Carbon-13 ($^{13}_{6}C$): 6 protons, 7 neutrons. Stable (1.1%)
- Carbon-14 ($^{14}_{6}C$): 6 protons, 8 neutrons. Radioactive (half-life 5,730 years). Used in radiocarbon dating

**Uranium isotopes:**
- Uranium-235 ($^{235}_{92}U$): 92 protons, 143 neutrons. Fissile (used in nuclear reactors and weapons)
- Uranium-238 ($^{238}_{92}U$): 92 protons, 146 neutrons. Most abundant (99.3%), less fissile

**Hydrogen isotopes:**
- Protium ($^{1}_{1}H$): 1 proton, 0 neutrons. Most common
- Deuterium ($^{2}_{1}H$ or D): 1 proton, 1 neutron. Stable
- Tritium ($^{3}_{1}H$ or T): 1 proton, 2 neutrons. Radioactive

### Atomic Mass

The **atomic mass** listed on the periodic table is the **weighted average** of the naturally occurring isotopes of an element, calculated based on their relative abundances.

$$\text{Atomic mass} = \sum (\text{mass of isotope} \times \text{relative abundance})$$

For example, chlorine has two major isotopes:
- Cl-35 (75.77% abundance, mass = 35 amu)
- Cl-37 (24.23% abundance, mass = 37 amu)

Atomic mass of Cl = (35 × 0.7577) + (37 × 0.2423) = 35.45 amu

This explains why chlorine's atomic mass is 35.45, not a whole number.

## Electron Configuration

### Fundamental Principles

The arrangement of electrons in atoms is governed by three key principles:

#### 1. Aufbau Principle (Building-Up Principle)

Electrons fill orbitals starting with the **lowest energy orbitals first**, then progressively fill higher energy orbitals. The order of filling is determined by increasing values of n + l (principal quantum number plus azimuthal quantum number), and for equal n + l values, lower n takes precedence.

**Order of orbital filling:**
1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → **4s** → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s → 5f → 6d → 7p

**Note:** The 4s orbital fills before 3d because 4s has lower energy. However, once electrons enter the 3d orbitals, they become more stabilized than 4s, which is why the 4s electrons are removed first during ionization.

#### 2. Pauli Exclusion Principle

**No two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers.** In practical terms, this means **each orbital can hold a maximum of 2 electrons**, and they must have opposite spins (one spin-up ↑, one spin-down ↓).

**Orbital capacities:**
- s orbital: maximum 2 electrons
- p orbital: maximum 6 electrons (three orbitals × 2 electrons each)
- d orbital: maximum 10 electrons (five orbitals × 2 electrons each)
- f orbital: maximum 14 electrons (seven orbitals × 2 electrons each)

#### 3. Hund's Rule

**Electrons prefer to occupy separate orbitals of the same subshell with parallel spins (same spin direction) before pairing occurs.** This is because unpaired electrons in separate orbitals experience less electron-electron repulsion than paired electrons in the same orbital.

**Consequence:** Half-filled and fully-filled subshells are particularly stable.

### Writing Electron Configurations

Electron configuration is written by listing orbitals in order of filling, with the number of electrons in each orbital shown as a superscript.

### Example: Electron Configuration of Sulfur (Z = 16)

Sulfur has 16 electrons. Following the Aufbau principle:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴

Or written in shorthand using the noble gas core:

[Ne] 3s² 3p⁴

Where [Ne] represents the electron configuration of neon (1s² 2s² 2p⁶).

### Example: Electron Configuration of Iron (Z = 26)

Iron has 26 electrons:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s² 3d⁶

Or: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶

Where [Ar] represents 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶.

### Orbital Stability Exceptions

The Aufbau principle provides the general filling order, but two important exceptions occur due to the extra stability of half-filled (d⁵) and fully-filled (d¹⁰) d subshells:

#### Chromium (Cr, Z = 24)

**Expected configuration:** [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s²

**Actual configuration:** [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹

Chromium moves one electron from 4s to 3d to achieve a stable half-filled d subshell (d⁵). This is energetically more favorable.

#### Copper (Cu, Z = 29)

**Expected configuration:** [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s²

**Actual configuration:** [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹

Copper moves one electron from 4s to 3d to achieve a stable fully-filled d subshell (d¹⁰). Similarly, elements like silver (Ag) and gold (Au) follow this pattern.

These exceptions highlight that **fully-filled and half-filled subshells are more stable than partially-filled ones**.

## Periodic Trends

The periodic table organizes elements by atomic number and groups elements with similar chemical properties. Several important properties vary systematically across periods and down groups.

### 1. Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** is a measure of an atom's ability to attract electrons in a chemical bond.

**Trend:**
- **Increases going right** across a period (more nuclear charge, same electron shell)
- **Increases going up** a group (fewer electron shells, electrons closer to nucleus)
- **Most electronegative element:** Fluorine (F) in the upper right corner of the periodic table
- **Least electronegative element:** Francium (Fr) in the lower left corner

**Explanation:** Atoms on the right have more protons pulling on the same number of electron shells, making them better electron attractors. Atoms higher up have fewer shells, so valence electrons are closer to the nucleus and more strongly attracted.

### 2. Atomic Radius

**Atomic radius** is half the distance between the nuclei of two adjacent atoms bonded together.

**Trend:**
- **Decreases going right** across a period (more protons attract electrons more strongly, pulling the electron cloud closer)
- **Increases going down** a group (additional electron shells farther from the nucleus)
- **Largest atoms:** Lower left (alkali metals, particularly francium)
- **Smallest atoms:** Upper right (halogens and noble gases, particularly fluorine)

**Explanation:** The periodic trend in atomic radius reflects the balance between the number of protons (increasing left to right in a period) and the number of electron shells (increasing top to bottom in a group).

### 3. Ionization Energy

**Ionization energy (IE)** is the energy required to remove one electron from a neutral atom to form a cation.

$$\text{Atom} + \text{energy} \rightarrow \text{Atom}^{+} + e^{-}$$

**Trend:**
- **Increases going right** across a period (smaller atoms with higher nuclear charge hold electrons more tightly)
- **Increases going up** a group (smaller atoms with closer valence electrons)
- **Highest ionization energy:** Noble gases (full valence shell, very stable)
- **Lowest ionization energy:** Alkali metals (one valence electron, easily removed)

**First vs. Successive Ionization Energies:** The first ionization energy (IE₁) is the energy to remove the first electron. The second ionization energy (IE₂) is significantly higher because the remaining electrons experience a higher effective nuclear charge. A dramatic jump in ionization energy occurs when removing an electron from a filled shell.

### 4. Electron Affinity

**Electron affinity** is the energy released (exothermic, negative ΔH) or absorbed (endothermic, positive ΔH) when an electron is added to a neutral atom.

$$\text{Atom} + e^{-} \rightarrow \text{Atom}^{-} + \text{energy}$$

**General trend:**
- **Tends to increase going right** across a period (more nuclear charge attracts the added electron)
- **Less predictable going down a group** (larger atomic radius makes it harder to add electrons despite higher nuclear charge)
- **Halogen** (Group 17) elements have high electron affinities (one electron away from stable noble gas configuration)

## Organization of the Periodic Table

### Groups and Periods

**Groups (columns):** Elements in the same group have the same number of valence electrons and exhibit similar chemical properties. Group numbers (1-18, modern IUPAC system) correlate to the number of valence electrons.

**Periods (rows):** Elements in the same period have the same number of electron shells.

### Element Categories

**Metals (left side):**
- Located on the left 2/3 of the periodic table
- Characteristics: malleable, ductile, good conductors of electricity and heat, lose electrons to form cations
- Include main group metals, transition metals, and lanthanides/actinides

**Nonmetals (right side):**
- Located in the upper right portion
- Characteristics: poor conductors, generally gain electrons or share electrons, form anions or covalent bonds
- Include O, N, C, F, Cl, and noble gases

**Metalloids (staircase line):**
- Located along the step between metals and nonmetals (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te, Po)
- Characteristics: intermediate properties, semiconductors

**Noble Gases (Group 18):**
- Rightmost column (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn)
- Characteristics: full valence shells, extremely unreactive, stable

**Transition Metals (Groups 3-12):**
- Middle section of the periodic table
- Characteristics: variable oxidation states, form colored compounds, many are paramagnetic
- Electron configuration: nd subshell is being filled

## Key Formulas

$$A = Z + N \quad \text{(Mass number = atomic number + neutrons)}$$

$$N = A - Z \quad \text{(Neutrons = mass number - atomic number)}$$

## Worked Examples for UPCAT

### Example 1: Calculating Subatomic Particles

Element X has atomic number 17 and mass number 35. Calculate the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in a neutral atom of X.

**Solution:**

- **Protons:** Atomic number = 17, so 17 protons
- **Neutrons:** N = A - Z = 35 - 17 = 18 neutrons
- **Electrons:** In a neutral atom, electrons = protons = 17 electrons
- **Element identity:** Atomic number 17 = Chlorine (Cl), specifically Chlorine-35

### Example 2: Writing Electron Configuration

Write the electron configuration of Phosphorus (P, Z = 15).

**Solution:**

Phosphorus has 15 electrons. Following the Aufbau principle:

1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³

Or in shorthand: [Ne] 3s² 3p³

The 3p³ indicates three unpaired electrons in the p orbitals (following Hund's rule).

### Example 3: Orbital Stability Exception

Explain why Chromium's electron configuration is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ rather than [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s².

**Solution:**

The half-filled d subshell (d⁵) is particularly stable. By moving one electron from the 4s orbital to the 3d orbital, chromium achieves a stable configuration with d⁵, which is energetically more favorable than a partially-filled d⁴ subshell. This demonstrates that electron stability is not solely determined by the Aufbau principle but also by the extra stability of half-filled and fully-filled subshells.

### Example 4: Periodic Trends

Which element has the higher electronegativity: Sodium (Na) or Chlorine (Cl)? Explain.

**Solution:**

**Chlorine** has higher electronegativity. Both are in Period 3, but chlorine is further to the right. Moving right across a period increases nuclear charge while keeping the number of electron shells constant. Chlorine (Cl, Z = 17) has more protons than sodium (Na, Z = 11), so it pulls on valence electrons more strongly. Therefore, chlorine is more electronegative.

**Electronegativity trend:** Cl > Na

### Example 5: Ionization Energy and Electron Shells

Which requires more energy to ionize: a sodium atom (Na) or a potassium atom (K)? Explain.

**Solution:**

**Sodium** requires more energy to ionize. Although potassium is below sodium in Group 1 (alkali metals), sodium has one fewer electron shell, making its valence electron closer to the nucleus and more strongly attracted. The valence electron of Na (3rd shell) is closer to the nucleus than K's valence electron (4th shell).

**Ionization energy trend:** IE(Na) > IE(K)

More generally: ionization energy increases going up and to the right, with noble gases being the highest and alkali metals being the lowest in their respective periods.

## UPCAT Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing atomic number with mass number:** Atomic number (Z) defines the element and equals protons. Mass number (A) is the total of protons and neutrons. Always use atomic number to identify an element.

2. **Forgetting that Cr and Cu are exceptions:** These transition metals have half-filled (Cr: d⁵ 4s¹) and fully-filled (Cu: d¹⁰ 4s¹) d subshells, not the expected configurations from the Aufbau principle. UPCAT loves testing this!

3. **Misunderstanding Hund's rule:** Electrons prefer to occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing. This is why nitrogen (N) has three unpaired electrons (1s² 2s² 2p³) in three separate p orbitals, not paired.

4. **Reversing periodic trends:** Remember: electronegativity and ionization energy increase going **right and up**. Atomic radius decreases going **right and up** (opposite direction). Memorize these visually.

5. **Confusing ionization energy with electron affinity:** Ionization energy is the energy to remove an electron (harder for atoms that hold electrons tightly). Electron affinity is the energy released when adding an electron (higher for atoms that attract electrons strongly).

6. **Not using noble gas notation:** Always simplify electron configurations using noble gas cores (e.g., [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹) to make calculations faster and clearer for UPCAT.

7. **Assuming all elements follow the Aufbau principle exactly:** The Aufbau principle is a guide, but orbital stability rules (half-filled and fully-filled subshells) override it in certain cases.

## Key Takeaways

- **Atomic structure:** Atoms consist of protons (nucleus, positive), neutrons (nucleus, neutral), and electrons (orbitals, negative). Atomic number = protons = electrons (neutral atom).
- **Isotopes:** Same element (same Z), different number of neutrons (different A). Isotopes have nearly identical chemistry but different masses and stability.
- **Electron configuration:** Determined by Aufbau principle (lowest energy first), Pauli exclusion (max 2 per orbital), and Hund's rule (unpaired before pairing). Watch for Cr and Cu exceptions.
- **Periodic trends:**
  - Electronegativity and ionization energy: increase right and up
  - Atomic radius: decreases right and up
  - Half-filled and fully-filled subshells are extra stable
- **Periodic table organization:** Groups share valence electrons; periods share electron shell count. Metals left, nonmetals right, transition metals middle.
