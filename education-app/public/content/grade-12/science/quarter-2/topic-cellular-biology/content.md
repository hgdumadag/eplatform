# Cellular Biology

## Introduction

The cell is the fundamental unit of all living organisms. Understanding cellular structure and function is essential for success on the UPCAT and for comprehending modern biology. In this module, we will explore the diversity of cells, from the relatively simple prokaryotic bacteria to the complex eukaryotic cells found in plants and animals. We will examine the specialized organelles that compartmentalize cellular activities, investigate how cells control what enters and leaves across their membranes, and master the precise choreography of mitosis—the process by which cells divide to create new life.

The topics covered here form the foundation for all subsequent study in molecular biology, genetics, and physiology. Pay special attention to the structural differences between prokaryotes and eukaryotes, the functional roles of organelles, and the mechanisms by which cells regulate their internal environment through membrane transport. These concepts appear frequently on the UPCAT in both straightforward recall questions and challenging scenario-based problems.

## Prokaryotic vs. Eukaryotic Cells

Cells are classified into two broad categories based on the presence or absence of a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles. This distinction is one of the most fundamental divisions in the biological world.

### Prokaryotic Cells

Prokaryotic cells lack a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Despite this structural simplicity, they are highly efficient and have evolved to thrive in virtually every environment on Earth.

**Key characteristics of prokaryotic cells:**
- **Size range:** 1–10 micrometers ($\mu m$) in diameter (much smaller than eukaryotic cells)
- **Nucleus:** Absent; genetic material is concentrated in a region called the nucleoid, which is not enclosed by a membrane
- **DNA organization:** Circular chromosome; exists as a supercoiled loop in the cytoplasm; may also contain plasmids (small, circular DNA molecules)
- **Membrane-bound organelles:** None; all metabolic processes occur directly in the cytoplasm
- **Cell wall:** Present in bacteria (composed of peptidoglycan) and archaea (composed of various materials); provides structural support and protection
- **Ribosomes:** 70S (smaller than eukaryotic ribosomes); used for protein synthesis
- **Examples:** Bacteria (E. coli, Streptococcus) and Archaea (thermophilic bacteria that survive in extreme conditions)

Despite their simplicity, prokaryotic cells possess a cell membrane (plasma membrane), cytoplasm, and ribosomes—the basic machinery necessary for life.

### Eukaryotic Cells

Eukaryotic cells contain a membrane-bound nucleus that encloses their genetic material, as well as numerous membrane-bound organelles that specialize in specific cellular functions. This compartmentalization allows for sophisticated regulation and efficiency.

**Key characteristics of eukaryotic cells:**
- **Size range:** 10–100 micrometers ($\mu m$) in diameter; generally much larger than prokaryotic cells
- **Nucleus:** Present; enclosed by a double membrane (nuclear envelope) with nuclear pores that regulate transport of materials
- **DNA organization:** Linear chromosomes; organized with histone proteins into a complex structure; located within the nucleus
- **Membrane-bound organelles:** Multiple structures including mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes, and (in plants) chloroplasts
- **Cell wall:** Present in plant cells (cellulose) and fungal cells (chitin); absent in animal cells
- **Ribosomes:** 80S (larger than prokaryotic ribosomes); still used for protein synthesis
- **Examples:** Animals, plants, fungi, and protists

### Similarities Between Prokaryotic and Eukaryotic Cells

Despite their differences, all cells—whether prokaryotic or eukaryotic—share fundamental features:
- **Cell membrane:** A phospholipid bilayer that controls what enters and exits the cell
- **Cytoplasm:** A gel-like substance containing the cell's contents
- **DNA:** Genetic material that encodes instructions for life
- **Ribosomes:** Structures for protein synthesis
- **Metabolic machinery:** Enzymes and cofactors for energy production and biosynthesis

## Cell Organelles and Their Functions

Eukaryotic cells contain numerous organelles, each specialized for a specific cellular function. Understanding the structure and role of each organelle is critical for UPCAT success.

### The Nucleus

The nucleus is the command center of the eukaryotic cell, containing the genetic blueprints for all cellular activities.

- **Function:** Stores and protects DNA; regulates gene expression; controls cell growth, metabolism, and division
- **Structure:** Double membrane (nuclear envelope) with nuclear pores; contains chromatin (DNA + proteins) and a nucleolus
- **Nuclear envelope:** Selectively permeable; allows specific molecules to enter and exit through nuclear pores
- **Nucleolus:** A region within the nucleus where ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is synthesized and ribosomal subunits are assembled

### The Nucleolus

- **Function:** Manufactures ribosomal subunits (40S and 60S subunits that combine to form 80S ribosomes)
- **Location:** Inside the nucleus; not membrane-bound
- **Activity:** Most active in cells with high protein synthesis rates

### Mitochondria

The mitochondrion is often called the "powerhouse" of the cell because it is the primary site of ATP (adenosine triphosphate) production through cellular respiration.

- **Function:** Produces energy (ATP) for the cell through the citric acid cycle and oxidative phosphorylation
- **Structure:** Double membrane (outer membrane and inner membrane); inner membrane is highly folded into cristae, which increases surface area for reactions; contains its own DNA (mtDNA) and ribosomes
- **Location:** Cytoplasm of virtually all eukaryotic cells
- **Significance:** Cells with high energy demands (muscle cells, liver cells, sperm cells) contain many more mitochondria than low-energy cells
- **Evolutionary note:** Mitochondria are thought to have originated from endosymbiotic prokaryotes; this is evidence for the endosymbiotic theory

### Ribosomes

Ribosomes are the cellular structures responsible for synthesizing proteins based on messenger RNA (mRNA) instructions.

- **Function:** Translation of mRNA into protein chains; site of protein synthesis
- **Structure:** Composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and ribosomal proteins; consists of large and small subunits
- **Prokaryotic ribosomes:** 70S (Svedberg units); found freely in cytoplasm
- **Eukaryotic ribosomes:** 80S; found free in cytoplasm or attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum
- **Distribution:** Found in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells (universal feature)
- **Note:** Antibiotics like tetracycline can target bacterial 70S ribosomes specifically, which is why they can kill bacteria without harming human cells

### Endoplasmic Reticulum

The endoplasmic reticulum is a network of membrane-bound tubules and sacs that is continuous with the nuclear envelope.

**Rough Endoplasmic Reticulum (Rough ER):**
- **Appearance:** Studded with ribosomes on its outer surface (giving it a "rough" appearance under electron microscopy)
- **Function:** Synthesis and processing of proteins destined for secretion or for insertion into membranes
- **Products:** Proteins exported from the cell (hormones, antibodies, digestive enzymes), membrane proteins, and transmembrane receptors
- **Destination:** Products are typically transported to the Golgi apparatus for further processing

**Smooth Endoplasmic Reticulum (Smooth ER):**
- **Appearance:** Lacks ribosomes; smooth surface under electron microscopy
- **Functions:**
  - Lipid synthesis (including triglycerides, phospholipids, and cholesterol)
  - Detoxification of harmful substances (especially in liver cells)
  - Calcium ion storage and release (important for muscle contraction)
  - Steroid hormone synthesis
- **Abundance:** Abundant in liver cells and steroid-producing cells; extensive in muscle cells for calcium storage

### The Golgi Apparatus

The Golgi apparatus functions as the "post office" or "shipping and receiving center" of the cell, receiving proteins and lipids from the ER and modifying, packaging, and shipping them to their final destinations.

- **Function:** Modifies, packages, and sorts proteins and lipids; adds carbohydrate groups (glycosylation); produces lysosomes
- **Structure:** Stack of flattened, membrane-bound sacs called cisternae; has a cis (entry) face near the ER and a trans (exit) face
- **Process:** Proteins enter via vesicles from the rough ER → undergo modification → are packaged into transport vesicles → exit toward their destinations (plasma membrane, lysosomes, or other locations)
- **Importance:** Without the Golgi, proteins would not be properly modified and would not reach their correct cellular destinations

### Lysosomes

Lysosomes are membrane-bound compartments containing powerful digestive enzymes—sometimes referred to as the cell's "suicide bags."

- **Function:** Cellular digestion; breakdown of cellular waste, dead organelles, and pathogens; autophagy (self-digestion)
- **Contents:** Hydrolytic enzymes (proteases, lipases, nucleases) that function optimally in an acidic environment (pH ≈ 4.8)
- **Origin:** Produced by the Golgi apparatus as vesicles bud off the trans face
- **Location:** Cytoplasm; more abundant in white blood cells and other cells that engulf pathogens
- **Note:** Animal cells contain lysosomes; plant cells typically lack them (instead using the large central vacuole)
- **Importance:** Protects the cell from its own digestive enzymes by compartmentalization; essential for recycling cellular components

### Vacuoles

Vacuoles are membrane-bound storage compartments that vary in size and function depending on cell type.

**Plant cells:**
- **Central vacuole:** Large, occupies 80–90% of the cell volume; stores water, nutrients, and waste products
- **Functions:** Maintains turgor pressure (keeps the plant rigid and upright); regulates internal osmotic balance; stores pigments (giving plants their colors)
- **Osmotic importance:** When a plant cell is placed in hypotonic solution, water enters the vacuole, creating turgor pressure that keeps the plant firm and leaves crisp

**Animal cells:**
- **Size and number:** Small and numerous; occupy only 5–10% of cell volume
- **Function:** Temporary storage of water, ions, and nutrients; participation in exocytosis and endocytosis
- **Less prominent:** Because animal cells lack the large central vacuole, they must regulate osmotic balance more carefully

### Cell Wall

The cell wall is a rigid, protective outer layer found in plants, fungi, and bacteria, but notably absent in animal cells.

**Plant cell wall:**
- **Composition:** Primarily cellulose (a polysaccharide polymer of glucose)
- **Function:** Provides structural support; protects cell from osmotic lysis; allows cell to achieve large size; prevents excessive water loss
- **Permeability:** Fully permeable to small molecules and even large proteins

**Bacterial cell wall:**
- **Composition:** Peptidoglycan (unique polymer of sugars and amino acids)
- **Function:** Structural support; protection; maintains cell shape

**Fungal cell wall:**
- **Composition:** Chitin (the same material in insect exoskeletons)

**Animal cells:**
- **Absence of cell wall:** Animal cells rely on the cell membrane and cytoskeleton for shape and support
- **Consequence:** Animal cells are more flexible and can undergo phagocytosis (engulfing large particles)

### Chloroplasts

Chloroplasts are the sites of photosynthesis in plant cells and some protists, where light energy is converted into chemical energy stored in glucose.

- **Function:** Photosynthesis; light energy → ATP and NADPH → glucose and oxygen
- **Structure:** Double membrane (outer and inner envelope); contains thylakoids (stacked into grana) and stroma (fluid-filled inner space); has its own DNA (cpDNA) and ribosomes
- **Location:** Cytoplasm of plant cells and some algae; absent in animal and fungal cells
- **Pigment:** Contains chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids for light absorption
- **Evolutionary origin:** Like mitochondria, chloroplasts are thought to be derived from endosymbiotic cyanobacteria; strong evidence for endosymbiotic theory

### The Cell Membrane (Plasma Membrane)

The cell membrane is the boundary that controls what enters and leaves the cell. It is selectively permeable—allowing some substances through while blocking others.

- **Composition:** Phospholipid bilayer with embedded and peripheral proteins; also contains cholesterol (in animal cells) and carbohydrates
- **Structure:** Hydrophobic fatty acid tails face inward; hydrophilic phosphate heads face outward toward water
- **Functions:** Protects cell; controls what enters and exits; site of cell recognition and signaling; compartmentalization in eukaryotes
- **Selectivity:** Small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, lipids) pass easily; large polar molecules (glucose, ions) require transport proteins or energy
- **Universal:** Present in all cells, both prokaryotic and eukaryotic

## Cell Membrane Transport

The cell membrane maintains homeostasis by regulating the movement of substances in and out of the cell. Transport mechanisms are classified as either passive (requiring no energy) or active (requiring ATP energy).

### Passive Transport

Passive transport moves substances along their concentration gradient (from high to low concentration) without requiring cellular energy.

#### Diffusion

Diffusion is the spontaneous movement of molecules from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration until equilibrium is reached.

- **Definition:** Random, continuous motion of molecules naturally leads to even distribution
- **Energy requirement:** None; entropy-driven process (molecules move to maximize disorder)
- **Examples:**
  - Oxygen diffusing from the lungs into the blood
  - Carbon dioxide diffusing from cells into the bloodstream
  - Perfume scent diffusing through a room
- **Rate factors:** Increased by higher temperature, larger concentration gradient, and smaller molecular size
- **Simple diffusion:** Small, nonpolar molecules pass directly through the phospholipid bilayer

#### Osmosis

Osmosis is a special case of diffusion—specifically, the diffusion of water molecules across a semipermeable membrane.

**Definition:** Movement of water from an area of low solute concentration (high water concentration) to an area of high solute concentration (low water concentration), driven by the tendency to equalize solute concentrations on both sides of the membrane.

**Key principle:** Water molecules move toward the side with more dissolved solutes because the solutes reduce the effective water concentration (water becomes less concentrated when dissolved particles increase).

**Osmotic solutions and cell behavior:**

*Hypotonic solution* (less solute outside the cell):
- Solute concentration outside < solute concentration inside
- Water concentration outside > water concentration inside
- Water enters the cell by osmosis
- **Animal cells:** Cell swells and may undergo lysis (rupture)
- **Plant cells:** Cell swells but does not burst because the cell wall provides structural support; becomes turgid (firm and rigid) from increased turgor pressure

*Hypertonic solution* (more solute outside the cell):
- Solute concentration outside > solute concentration inside
- Water concentration outside < water concentration inside
- Water leaves the cell by osmosis
- **Animal cells:** Cell shrinks; undergoes crenation (develops a shriveled, spiky appearance)
- **Plant cells:** Cell shrinks; the cytoplasm and cell membrane pull away from the cell wall in a process called plasmolysis

*Isotonic solution* (equal solute concentration):
- Solute concentration outside = solute concentration inside
- Water concentration outside = water concentration inside
- No net movement of water across the membrane
- **Animal and plant cells:** Maintain normal size and shape; this is the normal environment for most cells in the body

**Clinical significance:** IV (intravenous) fluids must be isotonic with blood (0.9% NaCl solution) to prevent crenation or lysis of red blood cells.

#### Facilitated Diffusion

Facilitated diffusion moves substances down their concentration gradient (from high to low) using transport proteins, but without requiring ATP energy.

- **Mechanism:** Substance binds to a carrier protein or channel protein; protein changes shape to facilitate passage across membrane
- **Energy:** ATP not required; movement is still along the concentration gradient
- **Examples:**
  - Glucose entering muscle cells via GLUT4 glucose transporters
  - Ions passing through ion channels
- **Specificity:** Transport proteins are specific for particular molecules
- **Saturation:** Can become saturated if all transport proteins are occupied (unlike simple diffusion, which is always linear with concentration)

### Active Transport

Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient (from low to low concentration) using ATP energy, allowing cells to accumulate essential nutrients and maintain ion imbalances.

- **Definition:** Movement of substances against the concentration gradient; requires cellular energy (ATP)
- **Energy source:** Hydrolysis of ATP provides energy for transport
- **Purposes:**
  - Accumulation of nutrients (glucose, amino acids)
  - Maintenance of ion gradients (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺)
  - Removal of metabolic wastes

**The sodium-potassium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase):**
- **Function:** Maintains sodium and potassium gradients across the cell membrane
- **Mechanism:** Uses 1 ATP to pump 3 Na⁺ ions OUT and 2 K⁺ ions IN
- **Result:** Accumulates K⁺ inside the cell and Na⁺ outside
- **Significance:** Essential for nerve signal conduction, muscle contraction, and maintaining cell size
- **Reversibility:** In extreme conditions (cell death), the gradient can dissipate

## The Cell Cycle and Mitosis

The cell cycle is the sequence of events from the formation of a cell to its division into two daughter cells. Mitosis is the phase during which the cell's nucleus divides, producing two genetically identical nuclei.

### The Cell Cycle

The cell cycle consists of three main phases:

**G1 phase (Gap 1):**
- Cell grows and accumulates nutrients and energy
- Organelles are replicated
- Normal cellular functions occur
- Cell checks for DNA damage and internal signals before proceeding

**S phase (Synthesis):**
- DNA replication occurs; each chromosome is duplicated to form sister chromatids joined at the centromere
- Histone proteins are synthesized
- DNA damage checkpoints are activated
- Cell contains twice as much DNA, but the number of chromosomes remains the same

**G2 phase (Gap 2):**
- Cell continues to grow and prepare for division
- Microtubules and other structures needed for mitosis are assembled
- Final checkpoint ensures DNA has been properly replicated and undamaged
- Centrosomes duplicate, creating two centrosomes that will form the spindle poles

**Mitosis:** Nuclear division producing two identical nuclei

**Cytokinesis:** Cytoplasmic division producing two daughter cells

Together, mitosis and cytokinesis constitute M phase.

**Note:** Interphase (G1 + S + G2) is NOT part of mitosis; mitosis occurs only during M phase. However, interphase precedes mitosis and is essential for its success.

### The Phases of Mitosis (PMAT)

Mitosis is divided into four sequential phases. A useful mnemonic is "PMAT" (Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase).

#### Prophase

Prophase is the first and longest phase of mitosis.

**Key events:**
- **Chromatin condenses:** Chromatin fibers coil and compact into visible chromosomes; each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at the centromere
- **Nuclear envelope breaks down:** The nuclear envelope disappears, allowing chromosomes to move throughout the cell
- **Spindle apparatus forms:** Microtubules polymerize from the centrosomes (located at the cell poles); microtubules attach to the centromere of each chromosome via the kinetochore protein complex
- **Centrioles move:** In animal cells, centriole pairs migrate to opposite poles of the cell
- **Nuclear membrane reorganizes:** Temporarily; reformed during telophase

**Duration:** Longest phase; takes about 5–10 minutes in a typical cell cycle lasting ~24 hours

#### Metaphase

Metaphase is the phase during which chromosomes are most condensed and easiest to observe—a favorite for UPCAT questions.

**Key events:**
- **Chromosome alignment:** All chromosomes line up at the cell's equator (the metaphase plate), forming a line parallel to the spindle poles
- **Spindle attachment:** Spindle fibers attach to the kinetochore of each chromosome; some spindle fibers attach to opposite poles (creating tension)
- **Maximum condensation:** Chromosomes are most visibly condensed during this phase; hence, they are most easily observed under a microscope
- **Checkpoint:** The spindle checkpoint ensures all chromosomes are properly attached before the cell proceeds to anaphase; if not, cyclin proteins are inactivated and division is halted

**Duration:** Shortest phase; lasts only 2–3 minutes

**UPCAT tip:** If asked to identify a phase of mitosis from a diagram, metaphase is easiest to recognize because chromosomes are lined up at the equator.

#### Anaphase

Anaphase is the phase of dramatic chromosomal movement.

**Key events:**
- **Sister chromatid separation:** The centromere proteins holding sister chromatids together are degraded; sister chromatids separate and are pulled toward opposite poles of the cell
- **Spindle fibers shorten:** Microtubules attached to kinetochores shorten, pulling chromosomes (now consisting of single chromatids) toward the poles
- **Opposing movements:** Spindle fibers not attached to chromosomes lengthen, pushing the poles apart and elongating the cell
- **Cytokinesis begins:** In animal cells, the cytoplasm begins to pinch inward at the equator (cleavage furrow forms); in plant cells, a cell plate begins to form

**Duration:** Relatively quick; lasts 2–5 minutes

**Result:** Each pole now has an identical set of chromosomes; the cell has twice the normal amount of DNA but still has the normal chromosome number at each pole.

#### Telophase

Telophase is the final phase of mitosis, during which the cell prepares to divide completely.

**Key events:**
- **Nuclear envelopes reform:** Double membranes reassemble around the chromosomes at each pole, enclosing them in separate nuclei
- **Spindle apparatus disassembles:** Microtubules depolymerize; centrosomes and other spindle structures disappear
- **Chromosomes decondense:** Chromatin fibers unwind from their condensed state, returning to dispersed chromatin within each nucleus
- **Nucleoli reappear:** Reappear within each nucleus as ribosomal RNA synthesis resumes
- **Cytokinesis completes:**
  - In animal cells, the cleavage furrow deepens and completely divides the cytoplasm, creating two daughter cells
  - In plant cells, the cell plate completely forms from the middle outward, eventually fusing with the existing cell membrane and cell wall

**Result:** Two daughter cells, each with an identical nucleus containing the same genetic material as the parent cell

**Duration:** Lasts 5–10 minutes; similar in duration to prophase

### Cytokinesis

Cytokinesis is the physical division of the cytoplasm, producing two separate daughter cells.

**In animal cells:**
- **Mechanism:** Contractile ring of actin and myosin filaments forms at the cell's equator; this ring contracts, pinching the cell in two
- **Cleavage furrow:** An indentation forms and deepens until the cell is completely separated
- **Result:** Two daughter cells connected briefly by a thin cytoplasmic bridge before complete separation

**In plant cells:**
- **Mechanism:** Cell plate forms from vesicles budding from the Golgi apparatus; these vesicles fuse at the cell's equator, forming a new cell wall and cell membrane between the two daughter cells
- **Cell plate:** Gradually fuses with the existing cell wall at the edges, creating a complete barrier
- **Advantage:** Plant cells retain their rectangular shapes and structural integrity

**Important note:** Cytokinesis typically overlaps with telophase, beginning during anaphase.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Identifying Organelles

**Question:** A researcher observes a cell under an electron microscope and sees many membrane-bound compartments containing digestive enzymes in an acidic environment. Which organelle is this, and what is its primary function?

**Solution:** This is a lysosome. Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes (proteases, lipases, nucleases) that function optimally at a low pH (around 4.8). Their primary function is to digest cellular waste, dead organelles, and pathogens through a process called autophagy. This compartmentalization protects the rest of the cell from the destructive enzymes.

### Example 2: Osmosis and Cell Behavior

**Question:** A red blood cell is placed in a hypertonic NaCl solution. Predict what will happen to the cell, and explain the molecular basis of this change.

**Solution:** The red blood cell will undergo crenation (shrivel). In a hypertonic solution, the solute concentration outside the cell is greater than inside. Water molecules move out of the cell by osmosis to equalize the solute concentration on both sides. As water leaves, the cytoplasm shrinks, and the cell membrane pulls inward, creating a spiky, shriveled appearance. This process is dangerous; excessive water loss can damage the cell irreparably.

### Example 3: Metaphase Identification

**Question:** A cell is shown in a diagram with chromosomes aligned in a straight line at the equator of the cell, with spindle fibers attached to the kinetochores. Which phase of mitosis is this?

**Solution:** This is metaphase. The key characteristic is that all chromosomes are aligned at the metaphase plate (the cell's equator) in a single line. This alignment is unique to metaphase and makes it the easiest phase to identify visually.

### Example 4: Comparing Transport Mechanisms

**Question:** A cell needs to accumulate glucose against a concentration gradient. Why can't this be accomplished through facilitated diffusion alone, and which mechanism must be used?

**Solution:** Facilitated diffusion moves substances only down their concentration gradient (from high to low concentration), even though it uses transport proteins. To accumulate glucose against its concentration gradient, the cell must use active transport, which requires ATP energy to pump glucose into the cell from an area of lower concentration to higher concentration. The sodium-potassium pump is an excellent example of active transport.

### Example 5: Cell Type and Organelle Abundance

**Question:** Why do muscle cells contain many more mitochondria than skin cells?

**Solution:** Muscle cells are highly metabolically active and require large amounts of ATP to power muscle contraction. Mitochondria are the organelles that produce ATP through cellular respiration. Cells with high energy demands (muscle, liver, sperm) contain proportionally more mitochondria than lower-energy cells like skin fibroblasts. The number of mitochondria reflects the cell's energy requirements.

## UPCAT Tips & Common Mistakes

1. **Prokaryotes vs. Eukaryotes:** Remember that prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus and organelles, while eukaryotes have both. The defining feature is the nucleus.

2. **Ribosome size:** Prokaryotic ribosomes are 70S; eukaryotic ribosomes are 80S. This is clinically important because antibiotics can target bacterial ribosomes without harming eukaryotic cells.

3. **Mitochondria and chloroplasts:** Both organelles have their own DNA and are thought to have originated from endosymbiotic prokaryotes. This is strong evidence for the endosymbiotic theory of eukaryotic cell evolution.

4. **Lysosome localization:** Lysosomes are found almost exclusively in animal cells; plant cells use the large central vacuole for similar functions.

5. **Cell wall composition:** Plant cells have cellulose cell walls; bacterial cells have peptidoglycan; fungal cells have chitin. Remember these specific polymers—the UPCAT tests this distinction.

6. **Osmosis direction:** Water moves toward the hypertonic solution (toward higher solute concentration). Don't confuse the direction: it's not based on water concentration alone but on the tendency to dilute the solute.

7. **Turgor vs. Plasmolysis:** In hypotonic solutions, plant cells become turgid (firm) due to increased turgor pressure. In hypertonic solutions, they undergo plasmolysis (cytoplasm shrinks away from the cell wall). These terms are frequently confused on exams.

8. **PMAT sequence:** Memorize the correct order: Prophase → Metaphase → Anaphase → Telophase. Metaphase is easiest to recognize because chromosomes are aligned at the equator; anaphase involves sister chromatids being pulled apart.

9. **Mitosis vs. Interphase:** Mitosis is only M phase (nuclear division). Interphase (G1, S, G2) precedes mitosis but is NOT part of it. DNA replication occurs during S phase, not during mitosis.

10. **Active transport energy:** Active transport moves substances against their concentration gradient and always requires ATP. The sodium-potassium pump is the classic example.

## Key Takeaways

- **Cell types:** Prokaryotic cells are simpler (no nucleus, no organelles) and smaller; eukaryotic cells are complex (nucleus + organelles) and larger. All cells share DNA, ribosomes, cytoplasm, and cell membranes.

- **Organelle functions:** Each organelle has a specialized role—the nucleus controls the cell, mitochondria produce energy, ribosomes synthesize proteins, the Golgi packages products, lysosomes digest waste, and chloroplasts capture light energy.

- **Cell membrane transport:** Passive transport (diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion) requires no energy and moves substances down their concentration gradient. Active transport requires ATP and moves substances against the concentration gradient.

- **Osmosis:** Water moves across semipermeable membranes toward the hypertonic solution (higher solute concentration). In hypotonic solutions, cells swell; in hypertonic solutions, cells shrink.

- **Mitosis (PMAT):** Prophase—chromatin condenses and spindle forms; Metaphase—chromosomes align at equator (easiest to identify); Anaphase—sister chromatids separate; Telophase—nuclear envelopes reform. Cytokinesis follows, dividing the cytoplasm.

- **Checkpoint regulation:** Cells have checkpoints before S phase, before mitosis, and during mitosis (spindle checkpoint) to ensure genetic material is intact and properly replicated before division.
