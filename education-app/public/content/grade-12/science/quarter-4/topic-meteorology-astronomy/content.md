# Meteorology and Astronomy

## Introduction

Meteorology and astronomy represent two interconnected branches of Earth and space science that examine dynamic processes shaping our planet and its relationship to the broader cosmos. Meteorology focuses on atmospheric phenomena, particularly the distinction between tsunamis and storm surges—two devastating natural disasters often confused by the general public but arising from entirely different mechanisms. Understanding these phenomena and the role of soil properties in flood risk is essential for disaster preparedness. Astronomy, by contrast, explores the solar system, stars, and celestial mechanics, including the mysterious concept of Earth's magnetic poles and their curious relationship to our geographic coordinates. For UPCAT, this topic demands precision in terminology, understanding of cause-and-effect relationships, and knowledge of fundamental properties of celestial bodies. This comprehensive review will equip you with the knowledge to ace questions on these diverse but equally important topics.

## Tsunamis vs. Storm Surges: A Critical Distinction

One of the most common errors in UPCAT is confusing tsunamis with storm surges. While both result in abnormal ocean water levels that flood coastal areas, their causes, characteristics, and warning signs are entirely different. This section will establish clear distinctions.

### What is a Tsunami?

A **tsunami** is a series of extremely long ocean waves generated by sudden, violent disturbances of large water masses. The key characteristic is that tsunamis are seismic or tectonic in origin—they result from energy released by geological processes, not atmospheric conditions.

**Causes of Tsunamis:**
1. **Underwater earthquakes** (most common cause): When the seafloor suddenly shifts along a fault, it displaces the water column above it, generating waves that radiate outward in all directions.
2. **Submarine landslides**: Underwater slopes that collapse and displace water.
3. **Volcanic eruptions**: Underwater or coastal volcanic explosions that displace water.
4. **Asteroid impacts**: Though rare, a meteorite striking the ocean would create massive waves.

**Characteristics in Open Ocean:**
In the open ocean, tsunamis appear deceptively small—often less than 1 meter high—but they travel at tremendous speeds of 500–900 km/h (comparable to jet airplanes). The wavelength is enormous, spanning 100–200 kilometers. Because the wavelength is so long relative to the water depth in open ocean, the tsunami passes beneath ships nearly unnoticed.

**Characteristics Near Shore:**
As a tsunami approaches shallow water, the seismic energy remains constant but is compressed into a progressively smaller water volume. The wave slows down (due to friction with the seafloor), the wavelength decreases, and the wave height increases dramatically—often reaching 10–30 meters or more. This increase in height as it approaches shore is called run-up.

**Warning Signs:**
A distinctive warning sign precedes the arrival of a tsunami: the water suddenly retreats from the shore, exposing the seafloor. This recession is caused by the trough (the part of the wave with low water level) arriving first. Residents who observe this sudden water recession should immediately evacuate to higher ground, as the wave crest will arrive within minutes to hours.

**Key Point: No Weather Required**
Tsunamis can occur during calm, sunny weather because they are caused by seismic energy, not atmospheric conditions. Weather has no influence on tsunami generation or propagation.

### What is a Storm Surge?

A **storm surge** is an abnormal rise in seawater level caused by the combined effects of low atmospheric pressure and strong onshore winds associated with tropical cyclones (hurricanes in the Atlantic/Eastern Pacific, typhoons in the Western Pacific, cyclones in the Indian Ocean).

**Causes of Storm Surge:**
1. **Low atmospheric pressure at the storm center**: Low pressure reduces the air's weight pushing down on the ocean, allowing the sea surface to "bulge" upward. This contributes relatively little to total surge height.
2. **Strong onshore winds**: As the storm moves inland or moves parallel to the coast, powerful winds (sometimes exceeding 200 km/h) push water toward the shore. Friction between the wind and water surface drags the entire water column ashore, creating a dome of water.

**Characteristics:**
- **Duration**: A storm surge can last for hours or even days while the tropical cyclone is offshore, as long as the low pressure and strong winds persist.
- **Appearance**: The surge appears as a gradual rise in water level, not as discrete waves. It resembles an abnormally high tide.
- **Extent**: Storm surge can penetrate far inland, especially in low-lying coastal areas and river mouths.

**Storm Surge in Action: Typhoon Haiyan (2013)**
Typhoon Haiyan (locally known as Yolanda) struck the Philippines in November 2013 with wind speeds exceeding 300 km/h. It generated a devastating storm surge that reached heights of 4–6 meters in Leyte and caused extreme flooding in coastal and near-coastal communities. The storm surge was responsible for most of the approximately 6,300 deaths, as families were swept away by the surge and subsequent flooding. This catastrophic event illustrated the lethal power of storm surge in regions with large coastal populations.

**Key Point: Always Associated with Weather**
Storm surges can occur ONLY when a tropical cyclone is nearby. They are entirely a meteorological phenomenon and have no connection to seismic activity.

### Comparison Table: Tsunami vs. Storm Surge

| Feature | Tsunami | Storm Surge |
|---------|---------|------------|
| **Cause** | Seismic (earthquake, volcanic eruption, landslide) | Meteorological (low pressure + strong winds) |
| **Nature** | Series of long waves | Dome of water; abnormal rise in sea level |
| **Speed** | 500–900 km/h in open ocean | Slower; travels with the storm system |
| **Wavelength** | 100–200 km | Not applicable (not a wave) |
| **Height in open ocean** | < 1 m | Often not visible as a separate phenomenon |
| **Height near shore** | 10–30+ m (run-up) | 2–6+ m depending on storm intensity |
| **Duration** | Minutes to hours from arrival to departure | Hours to days while storm is offshore |
| **Warning sign** | Sudden ocean recession | Gradual rise in water level with strong winds |
| **Weather required** | No; occurs in calm weather | Yes; always associated with tropical cyclone |
| **Can occur during calm weather** | Yes | No |

## Flooding, Soil Porosity, and Water Permeability

Understanding soil properties is crucial for predicting and managing flood risk. Two key properties determine how water behaves in soil: porosity and permeability.

### Porosity: Holding Capacity

**Porosity** is the percentage of pore spaces (voids) in a soil or rock. It represents how much water a soil can potentially hold. Porosity is determined by particle size and packing arrangement:
- **Sandy soil**: Large pores between grains → high porosity (30–40%)
- **Clay soil**: Small pores between fine particles → low porosity (20–30%)
- **Silty soil**: Intermediate porosity

Porosity alone does NOT determine drainage rate; it only indicates the total volume of water that can be stored.

### Permeability: Flow Rate

**Permeability** is the ease with which water moves through soil. It is determined primarily by pore size and pore connectivity:
- **Sandy soil**: Large pores with good connectivity → high permeability (water drains quickly)
- **Clay soil**: Tiny pores that are poorly connected → low permeability (water drains slowly)

The critical insight is that **a soil with high porosity but low permeability cannot effectively drain water**, leading to flooding.

### Porosity, Permeability, and Flooding Risk

**Scenario 1: Sandy Soil (High Porosity + High Permeability)**
When it rains on sandy soil, water quickly infiltrates (soaks into the ground) due to large, well-connected pores. Little water runs off the surface. Therefore, sandy soils have a LOW risk of causing surface flooding. The infiltrating water becomes groundwater, recharging aquifers.

**Scenario 2: Clay Soil (Low Porosity + Low Permeability)**
Clay soil has small, poorly connected pores that resist water penetration. When it rains heavily, water cannot infiltrate quickly enough, so water accumulates on the surface and flows downhill as runoff. This HIGH surface runoff increases flood risk significantly.

**Scenario 3: Impervious Surfaces (0 Porosity + 0 Permeability)**
Concrete, asphalt, and other human-made impervious surfaces (roofs, roads, parking lots) prevent any water infiltration. All rainfall becomes surface runoff. Urban areas with extensive impervious surfaces experience severe flooding from even moderate rainfall.

### Factors That Increase Flooding Risk

1. **Deforestation**: Tree roots normally absorb large quantities of water during rainfall and anchor the soil, reducing erosion. Removing forests eliminates this water absorption, increasing both surface runoff and soil erosion.

2. **Wetland destruction**: Wetlands act as natural sponges, absorbing water during floods and slowly releasing it. Draining wetlands eliminates this natural flood buffer.

3. **Urbanization**: Building cities replaces permeable soil with impervious roads and buildings, dramatically increasing runoff.

4. **Soil compaction**: Repeated foot traffic or vehicle traffic compresses soil, reducing porosity and permeability.

### Watersheds and Drainage Basins

A **watershed** (or drainage basin) is the area of land that drains into a common body of water such as a river, lake, or ocean. All rainfall within a watershed eventually flows to the outlet point, making watershed-scale management critical for flood control. Dams, retention basins, and forest conservation are managed at the watershed level.

## The Solar System: Organization and Components

The solar system consists of the Sun at its center, eight planets (and formerly nine before Pluto's reclassification), dwarf planets, asteroids, comets, moons, and other smaller bodies. Understanding the order of planets and their properties is fundamental.

### Order of Planets from the Sun

$$\text{Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune}$$

**Memory aid**: "My Very Educated Mother Just Served Us Noodles" (the last word changed after Pluto's reclassification in 2006).

### Classification of Planets

**Terrestrial (Rocky) Planets**: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars
- Small, dense, solid rocky surfaces
- Few or no moons (Earth has 1; Mars has 2; Mercury and Venus have none)
- Close to the Sun; high surface temperatures for inner planets

**Jovian (Gas/Ice Giant) Planets**: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune
- Large, low density
- Composed primarily of hydrogen, helium, water, ammonia, and methane
- No solid surface; weather occurs in thick atmospheres
- Many moons (Jupiter has 95+; Saturn has 146+; Uranus has 27; Neptune has 14)
- Extensive ring systems (Saturn's rings most prominent)
- Far from the Sun; low surface temperatures

### Other Solar System Components

**The Asteroid Belt**
Located between Mars and Jupiter, the asteroid belt contains millions of rocky bodies ranging from meters to hundreds of kilometers in diameter. Most asteroids are in the region between 2.2 and 3.2 AU (astronomical units) from the Sun. The asteroid belt is not densely packed; spacecraft pass through with negligible collision risk.

**Comets**
Comets are icy bodies composed of rock, ice, and dust. When a comet approaches the Sun, solar radiation heats its ice, producing a vapor that escapes as a tail. Remarkably, the tail always points **away from the Sun**, regardless of the comet's direction of travel, because the solar wind pushes the escaped gases. Famous comets include Halley's Comet (returns every 76 years) and Comet NEOWISE.

**Dwarf Planets**
In 2006, the International Astronomical Union redefined "planet" to require three criteria: (1) orbits the Sun, (2) has sufficient mass to be round due to gravity, and (3) has cleared its orbital region of other debris. Pluto fails criterion 3, so it was reclassified as a dwarf planet. Other dwarf planets include Eris, Ceres (in the asteroid belt), and Makemake.

**Natural Satellites (Moons)**
Moons are natural objects orbiting planets. Earth has one moon; Mars has two (Phobos and Deimos); Jupiter and Saturn have numerous large moons plus many small ones. Some notable moons include Earth's Moon, Jupiter's Io (most geologically active body in the solar system) and Europa (possible subsurface ocean), and Saturn's Titan (thick atmosphere) and Enceladus.

## Stars and Stellar Properties

Stars are massive spheres of plasma (ionized gas) held together by gravity and powered by nuclear fusion in their cores. Understanding stellar properties—luminosity, color, temperature, and life cycle—is essential for astronomy.

### Brightness and Magnitude

**Apparent Magnitude**: How bright a star appears from Earth. The magnitude scale is logarithmic and **inverted** (lower numbers = brighter stars). The brightest star in the night sky is Sirius (apparent magnitude ≈ −1.46), also known as the "Dog Star" (brightest star in Canis Major). The human eye can see stars down to about magnitude 6.

**Absolute Magnitude**: A star's intrinsic luminosity—how bright it actually is, independent of distance. Absolute magnitude is defined as the apparent magnitude a star would have at a standard distance of 10 parsecs (≈ 32.6 light-years) from Earth.

**Why the Distinction Matters**
A dim star close to Earth might appear brighter than an intrinsically luminous star far away. Apparent magnitude tells us brightness as seen from Earth; absolute magnitude reveals the star's true power output.

### Star Color and Temperature

Stars emit light across a spectrum, but their dominant color indicates their surface temperature:
- **Blue/white stars**: Extremely hot (> 10,000 K) → high luminosity
- **Yellow stars**: Moderate temperature (5,500–6,000 K) → moderate luminosity (like our Sun)
- **Red/orange stars**: Cooler (< 4,000 K) → low luminosity (unless giants)

Our Sun is a yellow dwarf, a G-type main sequence star with a surface temperature of about 5,778 K. Despite its modest size and luminosity, the Sun's steady fusion has allowed Earth's biosphere to thrive for billions of years.

### The Hertzsprung-Russell (H-R) Diagram

Astronomers plot stars on an H-R diagram with absolute magnitude (brightness) on the y-axis and spectral type/temperature on the x-axis. This diagram reveals the life stages of stars:
- **Main sequence**: Stars spend most of their lives here, steadily fusing hydrogen into helium. Our Sun is on the main sequence.
- **Red giants/supergiants**: Stars that have exhausted core hydrogen and expanded dramatically.
- **White dwarfs**: Compact remnants of dead stars.
- **Neutron stars and black holes**: Even more exotic end states.

### Star Life Cycle (Simplified)

1. **Nebula**: A cloud of gas and dust
2. **Protostar**: Gravity collapses the nebula; temperatures and pressures rise
3. **Main sequence**: Hydrogen fusion begins; the star stabilizes and shines steadily for billions of years
4. **Red giant/supergiant**: Hydrogen is depleted; core contracts; outer layers expand and cool
5. **Planetary nebula or supernova**: The star's outer layers are ejected
6. **White dwarf, neutron star, or black hole**: The core remnant depending on the star's mass
   - Low-mass stars → white dwarf
   - Massive stars → neutron star
   - Very massive stars → black hole

## Earth's Magnetic Poles and Geographic Poles

Earth's magnetic field is one of the planet's most important but least understood features. The interaction between magnetic and geographic poles often confuses students—but understanding it is critical for UPCAT.

### Geographic Poles

The **geographic North Pole** and **geographic South Pole** are located at the intersections of Earth's rotation axis with the surface. These are fixed points: the North Pole is at 90° N latitude, the South Pole at 90° S latitude. Geographic north is also called **true north** and is used as the reference direction on maps and compasses.

### Magnetic Poles

The **magnetic poles** are where Earth's magnetic field lines point vertically downward (for the North) or upward (for the South). They do NOT coincide exactly with geographic poles. Currently, the magnetic North Pole is located in northern Canada/Arctic Ocean, about 1,000 km away from the geographic North Pole. The magnetic poles also move over time (secular variation) and have reversed direction throughout Earth's history (polar reversals).

### The Key Insight: Magnetic Polarity Reversal

Here is the remarkable and often-missed fact: **Earth's geographic North Pole is actually a MAGNETIC SOUTH POLE**.

Why? A compass's north-seeking end points toward the magnetic field lines, which converge at Earth's magnetic poles. The north-seeking end of a compass needle is a magnetic north pole. Since opposite magnetic poles attract, the compass's magnetic north pole is attracted to Earth's magnetic **south** pole, which is located at the geographic North Pole.

Conversely, Earth's geographic South Pole is a magnetic **north** pole, repelling the compass's north-seeking end.

This counterintuitive fact is a favorite UPCAT trick question: "The north-seeking end of a compass points toward Earth's geographic North Pole. What does this tell you about the magnetic polarity at the geographic North Pole?" Answer: It must be a magnetic south pole.

### Magnetic Declination

**Magnetic declination** is the angle between true geographic north (from the geographic North Pole) and magnetic north (from the magnetic North Pole). It varies by geographic location. In the western United States, magnetic north is west of true north. Navigators must account for this declination when using a compass in relation to a map.

### Earth's Magnetic Field: Origin and Protection

Earth's magnetic field is generated by the convection of molten iron and nickel in the outer core. This geodynamo converts kinetic energy into electromagnetic energy, creating the field that extends far into space.

**Protection from Solar Wind**: Earth's magnetic field deflects the solar wind—streams of charged particles from the Sun—away from the atmosphere. Without this magnetic shield, the solar wind would erode our atmosphere and expose the surface to damaging solar radiation. Life as we know it could not exist without Earth's magnetic field.

### Aurora Borealis and Aurora Australis

**Aurora Borealis** (Northern Lights) and **Aurora Australis** (Southern Lights) are natural light displays in the sky caused by the interaction of charged solar particles (mostly electrons and protons) with Earth's magnetic field near the poles. These particles follow Earth's magnetic field lines toward the magnetic poles. As they collide with oxygen and nitrogen molecules in the upper atmosphere, they excite those molecules, causing them to emit light. The result is the dancing curtains of color—typically green, but sometimes red, blue, or purple—that have fascinated humans for millennia.

Auroras occur most frequently during periods of high solar activity and are most visible within the "auroral oval"—a ring-shaped zone around the magnetic poles at latitudes of 60–75° (which includes locations such as Alaska, northern Canada, Scandinavia, and Iceland).

## The Moon and Tides

Though the Moon is a single object, its gravitational effects profoundly shape Earth's oceans and indirectly influence life.

### Gravitational Cause of Tides

The **tide** is the periodic rise and fall of the sea surface caused primarily by the gravitational pull of the Moon and, to a lesser extent, the Sun. The Moon's gravity pulls on Earth's oceans, creating a bulge of water on the side facing the Moon. A second bulge forms on the opposite side due to the centrifugal effect of Earth's rotation around the Earth-Moon barycenter. As Earth rotates beneath these bulges, most locations experience two high tides and two low tides per day.

### Spring Tides and Neap Tides

**Spring Tides**: Occur when the Sun, Earth, and Moon are aligned (during new moon or full moon). The Sun's gravitational pull is aligned with the Moon's, causing the highest high tides and the lowest low tides. These are called "spring" tides not because they occur in spring, but because the water seems to "spring" up higher.

**Neap Tides**: Occur when the Sun and Moon are at a 90° angle relative to Earth (during the first and third quarter moons). The Sun's pull partially cancels the Moon's pull, resulting in moderate tides with smaller differences between high and low tides.

### Moon Phases

As the Moon orbits Earth, the angle between the Sun, Earth, and Moon changes, altering how much of the illuminated side of the Moon is visible from Earth. The moon phase cycle takes about 29.5 days (a lunar month):
- **New Moon**: Moon between Sun and Earth; fully dark (invisible)
- **Waxing Crescent**: Narrow lit crescent on the right
- **First Quarter**: Half lit; Sun-Earth-Moon at 90°
- **Waxing Gibbous**: More than half lit; approaching full
- **Full Moon**: Earth between Sun and Moon; fully lit
- **Waning Gibbous**: More than half lit; after full moon
- **Third Quarter** (or Last Quarter): Half lit on the left
- **Waning Crescent**: Narrow lit crescent on the left; approaching new moon

## Key Formulas and Equations

While most of this topic is conceptual, a few quantitative relationships are important:

- **Apparent magnitude to luminosity**: The magnitude scale is logarithmic; a difference of 5 magnitudes corresponds to a factor of 100 in luminosity.
- **Distance modulus** (for absolute vs. apparent magnitude): $m - M = 5 \log_{10}(d) - 5$, where $m$ is apparent magnitude, $M$ is absolute magnitude, and $d$ is distance in parsecs.

Most questions, however, focus on conceptual understanding rather than calculation.

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Distinguishing Tsunami from Storm Surge**

On December 26, 2004, a massive earthquake off the coast of Sumatra generated a tsunami that killed approximately 230,000 people across the Indian Ocean. A year later, in October 2005, Hurricane Wilma brought storm surge and flooding to the Gulf of Mexico coast. For each event, identify whether it was a tsunami or a storm surge and explain the cause.

*Solution*:
- **December 2004 event**: This was a **tsunami**. The cause was a massive undersea earthquake (magnitude 9.1) that suddenly shifted the seafloor, displacing the water column and generating the waves.
- **October 2005 event**: This was a **storm surge**. The cause was low atmospheric pressure at the hurricane's center combined with strong onshore winds that pushed water toward the coast.

**Example 2: Soil Porosity and Flooding**

Two identical 10 cm³ soil samples are collected: one sandy soil and one clay soil. When 5 mL of water is poured on each, the sandy soil completely absorbs the water within 2 minutes, while the water sits on the clay soil's surface. Explain why and predict which soil is more prone to causing flooding.

*Solution*:
Sandy soil has large, well-connected pores (high permeability), allowing water to infiltrate rapidly. Clay soil has small, poorly connected pores (low permeability), preventing rapid infiltration. Therefore, clay soil allows water to accumulate on the surface and run off downhill, increasing flood risk. Sandy soil reduces flooding by allowing infiltration.

**Example 3: Ordering Planets**

List the planets in order from nearest to farthest from the Sun, and identify which are terrestrial and which are Jovian.

*Solution*:
Terrestrial (nearest to farthest): Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars
Jovian (farther from Sun): Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune

**Example 4: Identifying Stars by Brightness**

Star A has an apparent magnitude of +5, and Star B has an apparent magnitude of +10. Which star appears brighter from Earth, and why?

*Solution*:
Star A appears brighter. The magnitude scale is inverted: lower numbers indicate brighter objects. Star A (magnitude +5) is brighter than Star B (magnitude +10). The 5-magnitude difference corresponds to Star A being about 100 times brighter than Star B in terms of light reaching Earth (though both factors—luminosity and distance—affect apparent magnitude).

**Example 5: Earth's Magnetic Poles**

A compass needle points toward Earth's geographic North Pole. If the north-seeking end of the compass points in this direction, what can you conclude about the magnetic polarity at the geographic North Pole?

*Solution*:
The north-seeking end of a compass is a magnetic north pole. Since opposite magnetic poles attract, the compass's magnetic north pole is attracted toward Earth's magnetic **south** pole. Therefore, Earth's geographic North Pole must be a **magnetic south pole**. This counterintuitive fact confuses many students but is a favorite UPCAT test item.

## UPCAT Tips and Common Mistakes

1. **Tsunami ≠ Storm Surge**: The most common error. Remember: tsunamis are seismic; storm surges are meteorological. Tsunamis occur in calm weather; storm surges require a tropical cyclone.

2. **Water Infiltration Depends on Both Porosity and Permeability**: A soil with high porosity (capacity) but low permeability (drainage) still causes flooding. You need both high porosity AND high permeability for effective drainage.

3. **Apparent vs. Absolute Magnitude**: Apparent magnitude is what you see from Earth; absolute magnitude is the star's true luminosity. Sirius is the brightest in the night sky (highest apparent magnitude) but is not the most luminous star overall.

4. **The Magnetic Pole Paradox**: Earth's geographic North Pole is a magnetic south pole. Expect UPCAT to test this counterintuitive concept with trick questions.

5. **Comets Always Point Away from the Sun**: The tail is pushed by the solar wind, not pointing from where the comet came. Even if a comet is moving away from the Sun, its tail still points away from the Sun.

6. **Moon Phases Are Not About Shadow**: Many students think phases are caused by Earth's shadow on the Moon (that would be a lunar eclipse). Phases are simply the varying angle between Sun, Earth, and Moon changing how much of the illuminated side is visible.

7. **Planets in Order**: Memorize the order perfectly. UPCAT will ask you to arrange planets or identify a planet's position. The mnemonic helps, but practice the actual names and order.

8. **Deforestation Increases Flooding**: Trees absorb water and anchor soil. Removing forests removes this protection and increases both runoff and erosion. This is a frequent UPCAT topic connecting geology, biology, and climate.

## Key Takeaways

- **Tsunamis** are caused by seismic activity (earthquakes, landslides, volcanic eruptions) and can occur during calm weather. They travel at extreme speeds in open ocean but increase in height near shore.
- **Storm surges** are caused by low atmospheric pressure and strong winds from tropical cyclones. They appear as a dome of water and are always associated with weather systems.
- **Soil porosity** (capacity to hold water) and **permeability** (rate of water flow) together determine infiltration rates. High porosity + high permeability = good drainage and low flooding risk.
- **Deforestation, wetland destruction, and urbanization** increase flooding by reducing infiltration and natural water retention.
- The **solar system** contains eight planets ordered Mercury → Venus → Earth → Mars → Jupiter → Saturn → Uranus → Neptune, divided into terrestrial (rocky, close to Sun) and Jovian (gas/ice giants, far from Sun).
- **Comets** are icy bodies with tails pointing away from the Sun; **asteroids** are rocky bodies; **dwarf planets** like Pluto orbit the Sun but haven't cleared their orbital region.
- **Star brightness** is measured by apparent magnitude (brightness from Earth) and absolute magnitude (intrinsic luminosity). **Sirius** is the brightest star in the night sky.
- **Star color** indicates temperature: blue/white = hot; yellow = moderate (like our Sun); red/orange = cool.
- **Earth's magnetic North Pole** (where field lines point down) is a **magnetic south pole**, located in northern Canada—NOT at the geographic North Pole.
- **Aurora Borealis and Australis** occur when solar particles interact with Earth's magnetic field near the poles.
- **Moon phases** cycle with an approximately 29.5-day lunar month. **Spring tides** (highest highs, lowest lows) occur during new and full moons; **neap tides** (smaller range) occur at quarter moons.
