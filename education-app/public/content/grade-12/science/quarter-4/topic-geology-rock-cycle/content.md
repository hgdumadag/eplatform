# Geology and the Rock Cycle

## Introduction

The rock cycle is one of the most fundamental concepts in Earth science, describing how rocks are continuously transformed from one type to another through various geological processes occurring over millions of years. Understanding the rock cycle requires knowledge of three main rock types—igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic—and how they interact through processes such as weathering, erosion, melting, crystallization, and metamorphism. This topic is critical for UPCAT, as it tests your ability to classify rocks based on their formation, describe the mechanisms of the rock cycle, apply the Law of Superposition to determine relative ages of rock layers, and calculate absolute ages using radioactive decay. By mastering these concepts, you will gain insight into how Earth's geology works and how scientists unlock the history of our planet.

## Rock Types and Classification

### Igneous Rocks: Born from Fire and Heat

Igneous rocks form from the cooling and crystallization of magma (molten rock beneath Earth's surface) or lava (molten rock that has reached the surface). The key factor determining the texture and mineral composition of igneous rocks is the rate of cooling.

**Intrusive (Plutonic) Igneous Rocks**

When magma cools slowly beneath Earth's surface, the magma chamber remains at high temperatures for an extended period, allowing mineral crystals to grow large before the rock solidifies. This slow cooling produces coarse-grained rocks with visible crystals to the naked eye. Examples include granite (composed of quartz, feldspar, and mica), gabbro (rich in dark minerals like pyroxene and olivine), and diorite (intermediate composition). Granite is particularly common and is used in construction due to its durability and aesthetic appeal.

**Extrusive (Volcanic) Igneous Rocks**

When lava erupts onto Earth's surface, it cools rapidly, sometimes within minutes or seconds. This rapid cooling does not allow time for large crystals to form, resulting in fine-grained rocks with tiny crystals or, in some cases, no visible crystals at all. Basalt is the most common extrusive igneous rock and is typically dark in color due to its high iron content. Obsidian is a glassy volcanic rock that forms when lava cools so quickly that atoms do not have time to arrange into a crystalline structure, producing a smooth, shiny, glass-like appearance. Pumice is a porous, lightweight volcanic rock that can actually float on water due to its high gas content.

**Key Distinction: Crystal Size and Cooling Rate**

The fundamental rule is simple: slower cooling produces larger crystals; faster cooling produces smaller crystals or a glassy texture. This relationship is so reliable that geologists can infer the cooling history of an igneous rock simply by examining its texture under a microscope.

### Sedimentary Rocks: Records of Past Environments

Sedimentary rocks form from the compaction and cementation of sediments—fragments of pre-existing rocks and minerals that have been broken down by weathering and erosion, transported by water, wind, or ice, and deposited in layers, typically in ancient seas or river systems.

**Clastic (Detrital) Sedimentary Rocks**

Clastic sediments are fragments of other rocks that have been broken apart by mechanical weathering. Based on particle size, clastic rocks are classified as follows:
- Conglomerate: coarse sediment containing pebbles and cobbles cemented together; looks like concrete
- Sandstone: medium-sized grains of quartz cemented together; often used as a building stone
- Shale: fine silt and clay particles compacted into a soft, layered rock

**Chemical Sedimentary Rocks**

Chemical rocks form when minerals precipitate directly from solution in water. Limestone (primarily calcium carbonate, CaCO₃) forms in warm, shallow marine environments and is often white or light-colored. Rock salt forms when seawater evaporates, leaving behind salt crystals. These rocks often form in evaporitic environments such as salt lakes and lagoons.

**Organic/Biochemical Sedimentary Rocks**

These rocks form from the accumulated remains of living organisms. Coal forms from the compacted and chemically altered remains of ancient plants that accumulated in swamps. Some limestone forms from the shells and skeletons of marine organisms that accumulate on the ocean floor.

**Key Features of Sedimentary Rocks**

Sedimentary rocks are characterized by horizontal layering (strata), often containing sedimentary structures such as ripple marks, cross-bedding, and graded bedding that indicate the direction and strength of ancient currents. Most importantly, sedimentary rocks are the primary rocks in which fossils are preserved, making them invaluable for understanding the history of life on Earth.

### Metamorphic Rocks: Rocks Under Pressure

Metamorphic rocks form when existing rocks are subjected to intense heat, pressure, or chemically active fluids deep within the Earth's crust, causing the minerals within them to rearrange and recrystallize **without the rock melting completely** into magma. If the rock melts entirely, it becomes magma and will eventually form an igneous rock.

**Foliated Metamorphic Rocks**

Foliated rocks have visible layers or bands of alternating mineral types, giving them a striped or banded appearance. This foliation results from the alignment of minerals under directional stress. Examples include:
- Slate: formed from the metamorphism of shale; splits easily into flat sheets used for roofing
- Phyllite: slightly more metamorphosed than slate; has a silky sheen
- Schist: formed from the metamorphism of sedimentary rocks; contains visible mica crystals aligned in parallel layers
- Gneiss: formed from the metamorphism of granite; characterized by alternating light and dark bands

**Non-foliated Metamorphic Rocks**

Non-foliated rocks lack banding and form from either uniform minerals or under uniform pressure from all directions. Examples include:
- Marble: formed from limestone; used as a decorative stone in sculpture and architecture
- Quartzite: formed from sandstone; extremely hard and often used in tools and construction
- Hornfels: formed from various rocks exposed to heat from nearby igneous intrusions

**Progressive Metamorphism**

As pressure and temperature increase progressively with burial depth, rocks undergo a series of metamorphic changes. For example, shale undergoes the following progression: shale → slate → phyllite → schist → gneiss, with each stage representing an increase in metamorphic grade (heat and pressure).

## The Rock Cycle: A Journey Without End

The rock cycle illustrates how all three rock types are interconnected and how rocks continuously transform from one type to another through geological processes. The cycle has no beginning or end; it operates continuously over millions of years.

### Pathways Through the Rock Cycle

**Igneous → Sedimentary**
When igneous rocks are exposed at Earth's surface, they are attacked by weathering (mechanical and chemical breakdown). The resulting fragments are transported by rivers and deposited in layers. Over time, compaction and cementation transform these sediments into sedimentary rocks such as sandstone or conglomerate.

**Sedimentary → Metamorphic**
As sedimentary rocks are buried deep within the Earth during mountain building or other tectonic activity, they experience increasing heat and pressure. Without melting completely, the minerals recrystallize and align, transforming shale into slate, limestone into marble, or sandstone into quartzite.

**Metamorphic → Igneous**
When metamorphic rocks are pushed even deeper into the crust or mantle, or when the temperature becomes extremely high, the rock melts entirely into magma. This magma can cool underground (forming intrusive igneous rocks) or erupt at the surface (forming extrusive igneous rocks).

**Any Rock → Magma**
Extreme heat can cause any type of rock to melt. Once magma forms, it will eventually cool and crystallize into igneous rock.

### Key Principle: No Melting in Metamorphism

A critical concept for UPCAT is the distinction between metamorphism and melting. Metamorphism occurs without melting; the rock remains solid and its minerals rearrange. If melting occurs, the process is no longer metamorphism—it is the formation of magma.

## Relative Dating: The Law of Superposition

Before the discovery of radioactive dating, geologists developed methods to determine the **relative ages** of rock layers—that is, which rocks are older or younger without knowing absolute ages in years.

### Law of Superposition

In a sequence of undisturbed sedimentary rock layers (strata), the layer at the bottom was deposited first and is therefore the **oldest**, while the layer at the top was deposited last and is the **youngest**. This principle is straightforward: objects cannot be on top of something older than themselves.

### Law of Original Horizontality

Sedimentary rocks are originally deposited in nearly horizontal layers. If you observe tilted or folded rock layers, something has disturbed them after deposition (e.g., tectonic forces, folding, or faulting).

### Cross-Cutting Relationships

An intrusion such as a dike (a sheet of igneous rock that cuts through existing rocks) or a fault (a fracture along which rocks have moved) is **younger** than all the rocks it cuts through. This is because the intrusion or fault could not have formed until after the rocks it cuts through were already in place.

### Unconformities

An unconformity is a gap in the rock record where erosion has removed some layers. An angular unconformity occurs when older rocks are tilted or folded, then eroded, and younger horizontal rocks are deposited on top. This surface of erosion is younger than the tilted rocks beneath it and older than the rocks deposited above it.

### Principle of Inclusions

A fragment of rock included within another rock must be older than the rock containing it. For example, if a granite pluton contains pieces of metamorphic rock, the metamorphic rock is older.

### Fossil Correlation

Certain fossils, called **index fossils**, are distinctive, widespread, and appear in rocks of only a limited time period. By identifying the same index fossil in rocks at different locations, geologists can determine that those rocks are the same age and can correlate rock layers across regions.

## Absolute Dating: Radioactive Half-Life

While relative dating tells us the order of events, **absolute dating** determines the actual age of a rock in years. This is made possible by the decay of radioactive isotopes.

### Understanding Radioactive Decay

Radioactive isotopes are unstable and spontaneously decay into more stable daughter isotopes over time. The rate of decay is constant and unaffected by temperature, pressure, or chemical environment. This makes radioactive decay an ideal "clock" for measuring time.

### Half-Life: The Key Concept

The **half-life** is the time required for exactly **half** of the original (parent) isotope to decay into the daughter isotope. After one half-life, 50% of the original isotope remains. After two half-lives, 25% remains. After three half-lives, 12.5% remains, and so on.

### Radioactive Decay Formula

The amount of parent isotope remaining after n half-lives is given by:

$$N = N_0 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$$

Where:
- $N$ = amount of parent isotope remaining
- $N_0$ = original amount of parent isotope
- $n$ = number of half-lives elapsed

Alternatively, the percentage remaining is $\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \times 100\%$.

### Calculating Age from Half-Life

Once you determine the number of half-lives that have passed, the age of the sample is:

$$\text{Age} = n \times t_{1/2}$$

Where:
- $n$ = number of half-lives
- $t_{1/2}$ = half-life of the isotope

### Important Isotopes for Dating

**Carbon-14 (¹⁴C)**
- Half-life: approximately 5,730 years
- Used for: organic materials such as wood, bone, and archaeological artifacts
- Effective range: up to about 50,000 years old
- How it works: Living organisms absorb ¹⁴C from the atmosphere. When the organism dies, ¹⁴C decays but is no longer replenished. By measuring the remaining ¹⁴C, scientists can calculate how long ago the organism died.

**Potassium-40 (⁴⁰K)**
- Half-life: approximately 1.3 billion years
- Used for: rocks and minerals containing potassium, including volcanic rocks
- Effective range: rocks older than about 100,000 years

**Uranium-238 (²³⁸U)**
- Half-life: approximately 4.5 billion years
- Used for: very ancient rocks, including some of the oldest rocks on Earth
- Effective range: rocks older than several million years

## Key Formulas

$$N = N_0 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n$$

$$\text{Age} = n \times t_{1/2}$$

$$n = \log_2\left(\frac{N_0}{N}\right) = \frac{\ln(N_0/N)}{\ln(2)}$$

(The last formula allows you to calculate n if you know the ratio of parent to daughter isotopes.)

## Worked Examples

**Example 1: Calculating Half-Lives from Remaining Percentage**

A fossil bone contains 12.5% of its original carbon-14. How many half-lives have passed, and what is the approximate age of the bone?

*Solution:*
- After 1 half-life: 50% remains
- After 2 half-lives: 25% remains
- After 3 half-lives: 12.5% remains

Therefore, 3 half-lives have elapsed.

$$\text{Age} = 3 \times 5,730 \text{ years} = 17,190 \text{ years}$$

The bone is approximately 17,190 years old.

**Example 2: Distinguishing Intrusive from Extrusive Igneous Rocks**

You observe two igneous rocks side by side. One has large, visible crystals of quartz and feldspar, while the other is very fine-grained and almost glassy. Which cooled more slowly, and why?

*Solution:*
The rock with large crystals cooled more slowly. Large crystals form only when magma cools slowly enough for atoms to migrate and arrange into ordered crystal structures. The fine-grained, glassy rock cooled very rapidly, not allowing time for crystal growth. The first rock is intrusive (plutonic); the second is extrusive (volcanic).

**Example 3: Applying the Law of Superposition**

In a cliff face, you observe the following rock sequence from bottom to top: sandstone, limestone, shale, and basalt. Assuming the layers are undisturbed, which layer is oldest, and which is youngest?

*Solution:*
According to the Law of Superposition, the bottom layer is oldest, and the top layer is youngest.
- Oldest: sandstone (bottom)
- Youngest: basalt (top)

The order of increasing age is: basalt < shale < limestone < sandstone.

**Example 4: Determining Metamorphic Rock Parent**

Limestone is subjected to intense heat and pressure during mountain building. What metamorphic rock will it become?

*Solution:*
Limestone consists primarily of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). When metamorphosed, limestone transforms into **marble**, a non-foliated metamorphic rock that is prized for sculpture and construction.

**Example 5: Tracing a Rock Through the Cycle**

A piece of granite (igneous rock) is weathered and eroded. Over millions of years, the resulting sediments are compacted and cemented into sandstone. The sandstone is then buried and metamorphosed into quartzite. If the quartzite is pushed deeper and melts, what type of rock will it become when the magma cools?

*Solution:*
Once the quartzite melts into magma and the magma cools and crystallizes, it will form an **igneous rock**. If it cools slowly underground, it becomes a coarse-grained intrusive igneous rock. If it cools rapidly at the surface, it becomes a fine-grained extrusive igneous rock. The rock cycle continues.

## UPCAT Tips and Common Mistakes

1. **Confusing Metamorphism with Melting**: Remember, metamorphic rocks form **without** melting. If a rock melts, it becomes magma → igneous rock.

2. **Crystal Size and Cooling Rate**: Larger crystals = slower cooling = intrusive igneous rock. Smaller crystals or glassy texture = faster cooling = extrusive igneous rock. Learn this cold.

3. **Superposition in Disturbed Rocks**: The Law of Superposition applies only to undisturbed rock sequences. If rocks are tilted, folded, or cut by faults, you must account for these disturbances.

4. **Half-Life Calculations**: When calculating the number of half-lives, remember that (1/2)^n gives the **fraction** remaining, not the percentage. Multiply by 100 to get percentage. If a sample has 25% remaining, that is (1/2)² → 2 half-lives.

5. **Index Fossils vs. All Fossils**: Only certain fossils (index fossils) are useful for dating because they are distinctive and limited to a specific time period. A fossil of a very common organism found in rocks of many ages is less useful.

6. **Relative vs. Absolute Dating**: UPCAT will ask whether you're determining a relative age (older/younger) or an absolute age (age in years). Superposition gives relative age; radioactive dating gives absolute age.

7. **Dating Limitations**: Carbon-14 is useless for dating rocks older than 50,000 years. Use Potassium-40 or Uranium-238 for ancient rocks. UPCAT loves this distinction.

8. **Rock Cycle Pathways**: Not every rock must follow every pathway. A rock can skip steps. For example, igneous rock can go directly to metamorphic rock if pushed deep underground without being weathered first.

## Key Takeaways

- **Three main rock types** exist: igneous (from magma/lava), sedimentary (from compacted sediments), and metamorphic (from existing rocks under heat and pressure).
- **Igneous rocks** range from coarse-grained intrusive rocks (granite) to fine-grained extrusive rocks (basalt) and glassy rocks (obsidian), depending on cooling rate.
- **Sedimentary rocks** form from fragments of other rocks and can contain fossils, making them records of ancient environments.
- **Metamorphic rocks** can be foliated (banded) or non-foliated, and form without melting.
- **The rock cycle** shows how rocks continuously transform between types through weathering, erosion, deposition, burial, melting, and crystallization.
- **The Law of Superposition** states that in undisturbed rock sequences, older rocks are at the bottom and younger rocks are at the top.
- **Radioactive half-life** is the time for half of a parent isotope to decay, enabling absolute dating.
- **Carbon-14** dates organic materials up to ~50,000 years; **Potassium-40** and **Uranium-238** date ancient rocks.
- Age calculations: Determine the number of half-lives from remaining isotope percentage, then multiply by the half-life to get age.
