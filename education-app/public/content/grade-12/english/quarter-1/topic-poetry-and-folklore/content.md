# Poetry and Folklore

## Introduction

UPCAT regularly tests your ability to read and interpret poetry—including Filipino verse from Tagalog, Cebuano, and other regional languages. Poetry is challenging because meaning is compressed into highly figurative, carefully chosen language. A single image can carry multiple layers of significance. A single line can shift the entire meaning of a stanza.

Why does UPCAT test poetry? Because poetry demands the highest level of reading comprehension. When you read narrative prose, the author often tells you explicitly what characters think and feel. But poetry requires you to infer meaning from compressed language, metaphor, imagery, and structural choices. This is the apex of reading skill—the ability to extract meaning from deliberately economical language. A person who can master poetry comprehension can master any text.

Additionally, poetry and mythology are foundational to cultural literacy. Understanding Filipino verse connects you to Philippine literary tradition. Understanding mythology—Greek, Roman, indigenous Filipino—connects you to the stories that have shaped human culture across centuries. UPCAT recognizes that educated graduates must be able to engage with these texts.

## Approaching Poetry on UPCAT: Five-Step Strategy

Most test-takers approach poetry randomly, rereading lines in confusion. Instead, use this systematic approach:

**Step 1: Read the Entire Poem Once for General Sense**

Do not stop at confusing lines. Do not obsess over unknown words. Read the whole poem straight through. Ask yourself: What is the general emotional tone? Is this joyful, sad, angry, nostalgic, ironic? What is the poem roughly about?

**Step 2: Identify the Speaker and the Situation**

Who is speaking in the poem? Is it the poet themselves, or a fictional character? What situation is the speaker in? What event or moment triggered this poem? Is the speaker alone or with others? These questions establish context.

**Step 3: Identify the Central Emotion or Dominant Feeling**

Poetry exists to communicate emotion and insight in compressed form. What emotion dominates the poem? Is it one clear emotion, or is the poem conflicted (mixing sadness and joy, for example)? This emotional core guides interpretation.

**Step 4: Reread the Poem, Focusing on Figurative Language**

Now that you understand the general terrain, examine specific language choices. Look for metaphors, similes, personification, imagery. Ask: Why did the poet choose THIS image rather than another? What does this comparison reveal about the speaker's state of mind?

**Step 5: Answer the Question by Returning to the Specific Line or Stanza**

If the question asks about a particular line, reread that section in context. Never answer a poetry question based on general impressions; always anchor your answer in specific textual evidence.

## Literary Devices in Poetry: Comprehensive Guide

Poetry relies on figurative language—language that deviates from literal meaning to create vivid images and emotional resonance.

### Metaphor

A metaphor makes a direct comparison between two unlike things WITHOUT using "like" or "as."

Example: "Life is a broken-winged bird that cannot fly." (Langston Hughes)

This statement is not literally true (life is not an actual bird), but it conveys the metaphorical truth that life is constrained, limited, unable to reach its full potential.

**Reading metaphor in poetry:**

1. Identify what is being compared (the tenor: "life") and what it is compared to (the vehicle: "broken-winged bird").
2. Ask: What qualities does the vehicle have that illuminate the tenor? A broken-winged bird cannot fly—it is grounded, limited, unable to soar. These qualities comment on the nature of life in the poem.
3. Never interpret metaphor in isolation. The surrounding lines provide context for what aspect of the vehicle is being emphasized.

### Simile

A simile makes a comparison using "like," "as," "as if," or "than."

Example: "O my love is like a red, red rose" (Robert Burns)

This explicitly compares love to a rose, inviting us to think about what roses and love have in common—beauty, fragrance, delicate petals, the thorns that accompany them.

### Personification

Personification gives human qualities to non-human things.

Example: "The wind whispered secrets through the trees."

Wind cannot actually whisper or keep secrets, but attributing these human qualities to wind creates an intimate, mysterious atmosphere.

### Imagery

Imagery is vivid, sensory language that appeals to sight, sound, smell, taste, or touch. Imagery creates a sensory experience for the reader.

Example: "The salt spray bit my face, and I tasted brine on my lips as the boat pitched beneath me."

This appeals to touch (salt spray), sight (implied by "face"), and taste (brine). Imagery makes abstract emotion concrete and tangible.

### Alliteration

Alliteration is the repetition of initial consonant sounds in nearby words.

Example: "Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers."

Alliteration creates musicality and can emphasize particular words or ideas.

### Assonance

Assonance is the repetition of vowel sounds within words.

Example: "Go slow over the road."

The repetition of the "o" sound creates a flowing, slow quality that mirrors the meaning of the line.

### Onomatopoeia

Onomatopoeia uses words that imitate the sounds they represent.

Examples: buzz, crash, murmur, hiss, pop.

### Hyperbole

Hyperbole is deliberate exaggeration for effect.

Example: "I've told you a million times."

The speaker has not literally told the listener one million times, but hyperbole emphasizes the repetition and frustration.

### Symbolism

A symbol is a concrete object, character, or action that represents an abstract idea. In poetry, symbols often recur across multiple stanzas, deepening their significance.

Common poetic symbols:
- **Light**: knowledge, hope, goodness, truth, clarity
- **Darkness**: ignorance, despair, evil, the unknown, confusion
- **Water**: change, cleansing, renewal, the unconscious, flow of time
- **Fire**: passion, destruction, transformation, purification
- **Journey**: life's progress, spiritual development, search for identity
- **Mirror**: self-awareness, truth, reflection

In poetry, the symbol's meaning emerges from how it is used in context. Always ask: "In THIS poem, what does this symbol represent?"

### Irony

Irony is the contrast between appearance and reality, between expectation and outcome, or between what is said and what is meant.

- **Verbal Irony**: Words mean the opposite of their literal meaning. Example: A soaking-wet person says, "Oh, what beautiful dry weather we're having."
- **Situational Irony**: What happens contradicts what is expected. Example: A lifeguard drowns.
- **Dramatic Irony**: The reader (or audience) knows something a character does not.

## Filipino Verse on UPCAT

UPCAT includes poems in Filipino languages, especially Tagalog. These poems may be presented in the original language with English translation, or in English translation alone. Understanding the context and resonance of Filipino verse is crucial.

### Common Themes in Filipino Poetry

**Patriotism and National Identity**: Filipino poetry often explores the nation's complex history, colonial experience, and struggle for identity. Poems may celebrate Filipino culture, mourn historical injustices, or assert dignity and pride.

**Family and Kinship**: The concept of "pamilya" (family) in Filipino culture extends beyond the nuclear family to include extended relatives and community. Poems often explore duty to family, love within families, and the tensions between individual desire and family obligation.

**Land and Place**: Many Filipino poems connect identity to geographic place—islands, mountains, rice paddies, coastal communities. Land represents home, heritage, and spiritual rootedness.

**Love and Sacrifice**: Romantic love in Filipino poetry often intertwines with themes of sacrifice, duty, and transcendence. Love may be portrayed as redemptive but also as demanding total commitment.

### Reading Filipino Verse on UPCAT

Even if you cannot fully translate a poem in Tagalog or another Filipino language, you can answer comprehension questions by:

1. Identifying repeated words and understanding their emotional weight
2. Recognizing the overall emotional register (positive, negative, bittersweet, ironic)
3. Noting structural elements (repetition, parallel lines, crescendo of intensity)
4. Reading the English translation carefully if provided
5. Focusing on the poem's themes and meaning rather than word-for-word translation

Example: The poem "Sa Pamilihan ng Puso" (In the Marketplace of the Heart) uses the metaphor of the heart as a marketplace where love, desire, vulnerability, and bargaining occur. Even without translating each line, you can recognize that the poem explores the transactional, risky nature of love—the way love makes us vulnerable to loss, the way we trade pieces of ourselves for connection.

### Example: Filipino Poetry Analysis

Consider this sample poem (translated from Tagalog):

"Linggo ng Ating Inang Bansa" (Sunday of Our Mother Country)

*The bells ring and we gather, old and young,*
*In the plaza our ancestors knew.*
*The stone church stands unchanged,*
*But we are not the people who built it.*
*We carry their names, speak their language,*
*Yet we are changed by years they did not live.*
*Still, on this Sunday, in this plaza,*
*We stand where they stood,*
*And something passes from them to us—*
*Not memory, but recognition.*

**Analysis**: This poem explores the theme of continuity and change, tradition and transformation. The physical settings—the stone church, the plaza—serve as symbols of unchanging history. But the repeated emphasis on difference ("we are not the people," "we are changed") acknowledges that each generation interprets tradition anew. The final lines suggest that what connects generations is not memory (which fades) but "recognition"—a moment of understanding across time. The tone is reverent and nostalgic but also accepts transformation as inevitable and even necessary.

## Mythology and Folklore on UPCAT

Myths and folklore serve educational and explanatory purposes. UPCAT tests your understanding of why cultures created these stories and what they reveal about human values.

### Aetiological Myths

Many myths are aetiological—they explain the origin of something. Greek myths explain natural phenomena (why seasons change, why certain plants exist), cultural practices (why we perform certain rituals), or human traits (why we are mortal, why we suffer).

Example: The myth of Persephone explains the seasons. Her abduction by Hades and her return to her mother Demeter account for the cycle of growth and dormancy in nature. This myth also comments on loss, grief, and compromise (Persephone spends part of the year in the underworld).

### Educational Purpose of Myths

Beyond explaining origins, myths teach values and lessons:

- **Greek myths** teach about fate, the limits of human power, the consequences of hubris (excessive pride), the nature of heroism, the will of the gods
- **Filipino indigenous myths** teach about relationship with nature, respect for ancestors, the consequences of breaking taboos, the interconnection of human and natural worlds
- **Universal themes** across mythologies include: the hero's journey, the cost of knowledge, the danger of defying cosmic order, the redemptive power of sacrifice

### Common Mythological Allusions in UPCAT

UPCAT may reference myths without retelling them fully. Recognizing these allusions requires familiarity with:

- **Achilles' heel**: A fatal weakness. (From the myth: Achilles' mother dipped him in the river Styx to make him invulnerable, but held him by the heel, leaving it unprotected. He was killed by an arrow to the heel.)
- **Pandora's box**: Releasing evil or irreversible trouble. (From the myth: Pandora opened a box against orders, releasing evils into the world.)
- **Narcissus**: Excessive self-love or vanity. (From the myth: Narcissus fell in love with his own reflection and was transformed into a flower.)
- **Prometheus**: Sacrifice for human benefit; stealing from the gods. (From the myth: Prometheus stole fire from the gods to give to humans and was punished eternally.)
- **Sisyphus**: Endless, futile labor. (From the myth: Sisyphus was condemned to eternally roll a boulder up a hill, only to have it roll down again.)

### The Concept of Eris in Mythology

In Greek mythology, Eris (goddess of discord/strife) represents conflict and discord. But eris in a broader philosophical sense represents the notion that opposites—chaos and order, light and dark, conflict and harmony—exist in tension and both are necessary for the universe to function.

When a text references "eris" or "discord," it may be suggesting that conflict is not merely destructive but is a natural, perhaps necessary force. This is more nuanced than a simple morality tale.

## Reading Poetry: Tone, Speaker, and Meaning

### Identifying the Speaker

In poetry, the speaker is not necessarily the poet. The speaker is the voice delivering the poem. A 60-year-old poet might write a poem in the voice of a child. A male poet might write in the voice of a woman. Understanding who the speaker is crucial for interpreting meaning.

**On UPCAT**, if you are uncertain about the speaker's identity, focus on what the speaker reveals through their words. What values do they hold? What is their emotional state? What do they desire or fear?

### Tone and Mood in Poetry

**Tone** in poetry is the speaker's attitude toward the subject, the reader, or the situation. Tone words include:

- Melancholic, nostalgic, wistful, elegiac (expressing grief or loss)
- Defiant, rebellious, resistant
- Reverent, awed, respectful
- Sardonic, ironic, mocking
- Tender, loving, compassionate
- Bitter, resentful, angry
- Triumphant, exultant, joyful
- Ambivalent, conflicted, uncertain

**Mood** is the emotional atmosphere the reader experiences. While tone is the speaker's attitude, mood is what the reader feels.

### Shifts in Tone

Poems often shift in tone. Watch for conjunctions like "but," "yet," "however," "although," "still," "though." These words signal a change in direction—a moment where the speaker's attitude shifts, often contradicting what came before.

Example:

*I loved him desperately, gave him everything,*
*Sacrificed my own dreams for his.*
*And yet—*
*I do not regret.*

The word "yet" signals a tonal shift. What appeared to be a lament becomes, paradoxically, an affirmation. The speaker's attitude changes.

## UPCAT-Style Worked Examples

### Example 1: Analyzing a Lyric Poem

**Poem: "The Last Light"**

*I stood at the window as darkness fell,*
*watching the sun slip behind the mountains.*
*My grandmother sat beside me, silent,*
*her hand wrinkled like autumn leaves.*
*"The light returns," she whispered.*
*"But we may not be here to see it."*
*I wanted to protest, to argue*
*against the quiet certainty in her voice.*
*Instead, I took her hand.*
*In that moment, I understood*
*that her acceptance was not defeat,*
*but a kind of courage—*
*the courage to see the darkness clearly*
*and choose not to rage against it.*

**Questions:**

1. Who is the speaker in this poem?
2. What does the "light" symbolize?
3. What is the grandmother teaching the speaker?
4. What shift in tone occurs in the line "Instead, I took her hand"?

**Answers and Analysis:**

1. **Speaker**: The speaker is a young or middle-aged person reflecting on a moment with their elderly grandmother. The first-person perspective ("I stood," "I wanted," "I understood") establishes an intimate, personal voice.

2. **Light symbolism**: Light represents life, hope, and the continuation of existence. The sun setting symbolizes the approaching end of life. The grandmother's statement that "the light returns" but "we may not be here to see it" emphasizes mortality—the world continues, but we do not.

3. **What the grandmother teaches**: The grandmother teaches that facing mortality with acceptance is not passive resignation but active courage. She has moved beyond denial or fear into clear-eyed acknowledgment of human limitation. Her "quiet certainty" is strength, not weakness.

4. **Tonal shift**: The poem shifts from the speaker's internal resistance ("I wanted to protest, to argue") to acceptance and understanding ("Instead, I took her hand"). The conjunction "Instead" signals this change. The tone moves from youthful rebellion against death to mature acceptance of human limitation. By the final lines, the speaker has adopted the grandmother's perspective—finding courage in clarity rather than false hope.

### Example 2: Identifying Figurative Language and Its Purpose

**Poem (original, UPCAT-style):**

*Her words were stones dropped into still water,*
*creating ripples that spread and spread,*
*touching the distant shore.*
*She spoke rarely, but when she did,*
*the world listened.*

**Question**: What is the primary effect of the metaphor "Her words were stones dropped into still water"?

A) It suggests her words were hard and cold
B) It conveys that her words were powerful yet simple, creating far-reaching impact from a single action
C) It indicates that she preferred to remain silent and withdrawn
D) It implies that her words were useless and left no lasting impression

**Analysis**: The correct answer is B. The metaphor of the stone and ripples suggests several things at once:
- The word itself (stone) is simple, solid, substantial—not ornate or complex
- The action of dropping it into still water creates movement and change from a single point
- The ripples spread outward, suggesting influence beyond the immediate moment
- The detail about touching "the distant shore" suggests that her impact reaches far beyond the initial audience

The phrase "She spoke rarely, but when she did, the world listened" reinforces this interpretation: her words are valuable precisely because they are not overused. She is economical with language. When the metaphor says the ripples "touched the distant shore," it suggests her words have lasting influence and wide reach.

Why not A? While stones can symbolize coldness, the context emphasizes impact and power, not emotional temperature.

Why not C? The metaphor does not suggest withdrawal; it suggests purposeful restraint—the impact comes from careful, deliberate speech.

Why not D? The spreading ripples explicitly indicate lasting impression and influence.

### Example 3: Interpreting Mythology and Educational Purpose

**Passage (simplified myth excerpt):**

> The goddess Anansi, keeper of stories, held all knowledge in a golden jar. The other animals asked her to share her wisdom. But Anansi said, "Knowledge is power. I will keep it hidden." One day, a young bird questioned her: "But if no one learns from your knowledge, what is its purpose? Power that serves only the powerful is not power—it is tyranny." Anansi considered this. She broke the golden jar, and the stories scattered to all creatures. From that day, wisdom belonged to everyone who sought it.

**Question**: What is the educational purpose of this myth?

A) To explain how birds learned to sing
B) To teach that hoarding power is self-defeating and that knowledge gains value through sharing
C) To warn against questioning authority
D) To show that the goddess was foolish to hold knowledge

**Analysis**: The correct answer is B. This myth teaches a moral lesson about the nature of power and knowledge. Anansi's initial stance is understandable—she believes controlling knowledge gives her power. But the young bird's challenge reveals a paradox: power used solely for personal benefit is hollow. It is "tyranny," not true power. The myth resolves when Anansi recognizes this truth and voluntarily shares knowledge. The lesson is that true power comes from generosity, from contributing to the community's wellbeing.

Why not A? The myth does not explain a natural phenomenon like bird song; it teaches a moral lesson.

Why not C? The myth actually encourages questioning—it is the bird's question that opens Anansi's mind.

Why not D? The myth does not portray Anansi as foolish; it portrays her as wise enough to learn and change.

## UPCAT Tips for Poetry and Folklore

**Tip 1: Never Answer Based on Personal Emotional Reaction**

Your personal feeling about a poem is irrelevant. You must answer based on what the TEXT says. If you found a poem sad, that does not make the correct answer about its theme something related to your sadness. Stick to textual evidence.

**Tip 2: For Figurative Language, Eliminate What It Clearly Is NOT**

If a question asks, "Which literary device does this line primarily use?", eliminate choices that are definitely wrong first. A line with "like" in it is not metaphor—it is simile. A line giving human qualities to an object is not irony—it is personification.

**Tip 3: Tone Questions: Identify 2-3 Emotionally Charged Words**

When asked about tone, scan the poem for words that carry emotional weight. Negative words create a different tone than positive words. Formal diction creates a different tone than casual diction. This quick analysis will guide you to the correct answer.

**Tip 4: For "What does [line] most likely mean?" Look for the Metaphor or Symbol**

Difficult lines in poetry often contain metaphor or symbol. Look for the image and ask what it represents. In context, the meaning becomes clear.

**Tip 5: For Filipino Poems, Focus on Emotional Register If You Cannot Fully Translate**

If you encounter a Filipino-language poem you cannot fully translate, focus on:
- Is the tone positive or negative?
- Are key words repeated (repetition emphasizes importance)?
- Does the emotional intensity build or remain steady?
- What is the overall message or feeling conveyed?

Even without perfect translation, you can identify these elements and answer questions about tone, theme, and meaning.

**Tip 6: Myths Teach Lessons Through Consequences**

When reading a myth or folkloric tale, pay attention to what happens to characters. Characters who defy the gods or ignore warnings face consequences. These consequences teach the moral lesson. The lesson is embedded in the story's outcome.

**Tip 7: Read Poetry Aloud in Your Mind**

Poetry is written to be heard. Rhythm, repetition, and sound matter. When reading a poem, "hear" the words in your mind. This helps you recognize alliteration, assonance, and the musicality that contributes to meaning.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Approach poetry systematically**: Read the whole poem first, identify the speaker and situation, recognize the dominant emotion, then examine figurative language.

2. **Literary devices create meaning**: Metaphor, simile, personification, imagery, and symbolism are not decorative—they are meaning-making tools. Understand what each device does and why the poet chose it.

3. **Tone reveals attitude; mood reveals atmosphere**: Identify emotionally charged words to recognize tone. Remember that tone can shift within a poem.

4. **Filipino verse connects you to cultural tradition**: Learning to read Filipino poetry in English translation deepens your understanding of Philippine literature and national identity.

5. **Mythology teaches universal truths through narrative**: Myths are not merely entertaining stories; they teach lessons about human nature, the consequences of actions, and cultural values.

6. **Evidence is everything**: In poetry, as in all reading comprehension, your answer must be supported by the text. Do not rely on personal interpretation alone.

7. **Close reading rewards careful attention**: Poetry demands that you read every word carefully. The poet chose each word deliberately. Your job is to understand why.
